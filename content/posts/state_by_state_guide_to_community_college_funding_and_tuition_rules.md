Community college is often described as the “affordable” pathway into higher education, but what students actually pay varies dramatically from state to state. In some states, community college tuition is close to zero for many residents, while in others it can be [several thousand dollars a year](https://www.aacc.nche.edu/2023/12/06/datapoints-students-college-costs-for-2023-24/) before any financial aid is applied, with average [listed prices for public two-year colleges](https://research.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/Trends-in-College-Pricing-and-Student-Aid-2024-ADA.pdf) rising and [significant regional variation](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights) in tuition and fees.

At the national level, the federal government provides a common baseline of aid available to students in every state, mainly through programs like the [Federal Pell Grant](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/pay-for-college/get-help-paying-for-college/scholarships-grants-institutional-aid/what-is-a-pell-grant) and [other federal student aid profiles](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2024_National_Profile.pdf). On top of that baseline, each state decides how much to invest in its own colleges through [statewide "promise" programs](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) and [specifically designed "free college" initiatives](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/). These choices, alongside available [need- and merit-based aid](https://collegefinance.com/financial-aid/guide-to-need-and-merit-based-financial-aid), shape what local students end up paying. Understanding this layered structure—federal first, then state, then colleges themselves—is the key to [navigating college affordability](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org) and making sense of why community college costs vary so wildly.

## How Much Community College Costs and Why It Varies


Community college prices start with a published "sticker price": the tuition and mandatory fees a college lists for a full year of enrollment. According to [recent College Board data](https://research.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/Trends-in-College-Pricing-and-Student-Aid-2024-ADA.pdf), the average published tuition and fees for in-district students at public two-year colleges is [around 4,000 dollars per year](https://nces.ed.gov/fastfacts/display.asp?id=76) nationally, but state averages [range significantly](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights) from roughly 1,400 dollars to [almost 9,000 dollars](https://www.aacc.nche.edu/2023/12/06/datapoints-students-college-costs-for-2023-24/). That means two students taking similar courses at two public community colleges could see list prices that differ by a factor of five or more, purely because of where they live.

On top of tuition and fees, students also face the broader “cost of attendance,” which includes books, supplies, transportation, and living expenses such as housing and food. [Federal data](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) show that at public two-year institutions, tuition and required fees typically [make up only about one-fifth to one-quarter](https://educationdata.org/average-cost-of-college) of a full-time student’s total annual cost of attendance, with the [majority tied to living costs](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/search/viewtable?tableId=35947). This is especially important when evaluating [“free community college” claims](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/), because most programs [target tuition and fees](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_330.40.asp), not the full [complexity of student expenses](https://nces.ed.gov/fastfacts/display.asp?id=76).

The large differences in published prices across states reflect policy choices about how much state governments subsidize their community colleges versus how much they expect families to pay. States that [provide higher general funding](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) to their community colleges can keep sticker prices lower, while states that invest less in institutional funding often [charge higher tuition](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights) and then may or may not offset those charges with [grants or scholarships](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org).

### Federal Financial Aid vs. State-Level Funding


Federal financial aid provides the same basic framework for students across the country, regardless of which state they live in. The central gateway is the [Free Application for Federal Student Aid, or FAFSA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NmEP38x-1Z8), which is the [form students complete](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/completing-the-fafsa) to apply for federal grants, federal student loans, and federal work-study. Colleges and many state agencies [rely on FAFSA information](https://www.snhu.edu/about-us/newsroom/education/why-is-fafsa-important) to decide what aid a student qualifies for, making it the essential [first step in accessing](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2024_National_Profile.pdf) both [federal and state help](https://finaid.org/fafsa/), as well as [institutional aid](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/report-your-data/faq-average-net-price).

One of the most important federal programs for community college students is the [Federal Pell Grant](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/pay-for-college/get-help-paying-for-college/scholarships-grants-institutional-aid/what-is-a-pell-grant), a need-based grant for undergraduates that does not need to be repaid. For the 2024–25 award year, the [maximum Pell Grant is 7,395 dollars](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/sites/default/files/2024-2025/2024-2025_Federal_Student_Aid_Handbook/_knowledge-center_fsa-handbook_2024-2025_vol7.pdf), with the exact amount depending on the student’s financial need and [overall cost of attendance](https://educationdata.org/average-cost-of-college). Because community college tuition is relatively low, [Pell Grants alone](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2024_National_Profile.pdf) often cover most or all of a [low-income student’s tuition](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) and required fees, at least before other aid is considered.

State-level funding adds a [highly variable second layer](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/) on top of this federal foundation. States may choose to utilize [statewide "promise" programs](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) or other strategies, such as:

-   Keeping tuition low through direct operating support to community colleges.
-   Offering need-based or merit-based grants that students can use at any in-state public institution.
-   Creating dedicated tuition-free programs that guarantee free or near-free community college for eligible residents.
-   Providing little beyond federal aid, leaving affordability to depend mostly on Pell Grants, loans, and institutional scholarships.

Because federal rules are relatively uniform, it is this [state layer](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org), as well as individual [college-level design choices](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/), that drives most of the variation in what students actually pay.
### Why Two Students Can Pay Very Different Amounts


Consider two students with similar family incomes and academic records who both attend public community colleges, but in different states. Both complete the FAFSA and qualify for the [same Pell Grant amount](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/pay-for-college/get-help-paying-for-college/scholarships-grants-institutional-aid/what-is-a-pell-grant) because federal eligibility is determined by a [national formula](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2024_National_Profile.pdf) that does not change by state. Despite that, their final out-of-pocket costs can [diverge sharply](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org).

First, their colleges’ sticker prices may be very different because of their states’ funding choices and tuition policies. Second, one state might supplement federal aid with [generous promise programs](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf) or [tuition-free initiatives](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/), while the other [meets fewer needs](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/). As a result, a Pell-eligible student in a state with [low tuition](https://www.aacc.nche.edu/2023/12/06/datapoints-students-college-costs-for-2023-24/) and [strong state grants](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) might pay close to zero for tuition and fees, while a very similar student in a [higher-tuition state](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights) could still face a significant bill even after Pell is applied.

These differences are not accidents of the system; they reflect each state’s policy priorities, budget constraints, and views about who should bear the cost of higher education. For families, this means that [“affordable community college”](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/more-states-offering-free-community-college/) is not a single national reality but a [patchwork of state-driven models](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) layered on top of [common federal programs](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/) and [individual financial circumstances](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org).

### The Rise of “Free Community College” Programs


Over the past two decades, states and localities across the United States have created hundreds of so‑called “college promise” or tuition-free college programs. Early high-profile efforts such as the [Kalamazoo Promise](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/what-are-promise-programs-and-how-can-they-help-make-college-more-affordable) helped popularize the idea that a community or state could guarantee tuition for local graduates, and the model has since spread widely. Recent research into [equity and opportunity](https://opportunityinstitute.org/research/post/college-promise-a-pathway-to-greater-equity-in-opportunity/) has documented more than 200 promise-style programs across at least 40 states, including over [20 statewide initiatives](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) and various [program design models](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/).

At first, most programs were local or regional, but in the mid‑2010s and early 2020s, an increasing number of states adopted statewide tuition-free community college initiatives. Recent reporting indicates that [around 30 or more states](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/more-states-offering-free-community-college/) now offer some form of free or near-free community college, even if the [specific rules and funding formulas](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) differ. This growth reflects a broader [national movement](https://www.calbright.edu/newsroom/blog/free-community-college-is-working-everywhere-but-its-just-a-start/) to raise educational attainment as states set goals for the share of residents with a postsecondary credential, though the [policy design remains varied](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/).

However, these programs are not standardized, and “free” does not mean the same thing everywhere. Some models cover only tuition and fees, while others [place caps on semesters or credits](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf). Understanding the different [funding structures](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/) behind these programs, and whether they truly [meet the needs of low-income students](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/), is essential before interpreting any state-by-state list of offerings.

## How State Community College Funding Works

### “Free Community College” Is Not Always Fully Free


When a program advertises “tuition-free community college,” it usually means that tuition and mandatory fees are covered for eligible students at specified public institutions. It generally does not mean that every cost associated with attending college—housing, food, transportation, books, and child care—is fully paid for, as many [“promise” programs focus exclusively on the tuition bill](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/what-are-promise-programs-and-how-can-they-help-make-college-more-affordable).

Federal and NCES data illustrate why this distinction matters. At public two-year colleges, tuition and required fees account for [roughly 20–22 percent](https://educationdata.org/average-cost-of-college) of the overall cost of attendance for full-time students, leaving the [vast majority of costs](https://nces.ed.gov/fastfacts/display.asp?id=76) tied to living expenses and supplies. Researchers analyzing [community college affordability](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) have estimated that [non-tuition expenses](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_330.40.asp) make up about [80 percent of what it truly costs](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/search/viewtable?tableId=35947) to attend, even when [statewide initiatives](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) are in place.

The term “net price” helps capture this reality. The U.S. Department of Education defines [net price](https://collegecost.ed.gov/net-price) as the amount a student actually pays in a given year after subtracting grants and scholarships from the total cost of attendance, not just from tuition. College Board guidance similarly emphasizes that [net price reflects what families “really pay”](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/pay-for-college/get-started/9-things-you-need-to-know-about-net-price) by factoring in all gift aid. A program can accurately claim to make tuition free while still leaving students with substantial costs once non-tuition factors are considered.

Programs are often marketed as “free college” because covering tuition is a powerful, simple message that is easier to communicate than a [nuanced description of aid layering](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/). For many students, especially those from low-income backgrounds, eliminating tuition bills is meaningful progress, but it does not [eliminate the need](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org) to [afford the basics](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) required to stay in school and [succeed as a local graduate](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/what-are-promise-programs-and-how-can-they-help-make-college-more-affordable).

### First-Dollar vs. Last-Dollar Programs

One of the most important design choices in state “free college” and promise programs is whether they are built as [first-dollar or last-dollar awards](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/). This determines how state funds interact with federal aid and can [dramatically affect who benefits most](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf).

#### First-Dollar Programs

A first-dollar program provides its scholarship or tuition waiver before taking federal and other grants into account. Under this model, the state covers tuition up front, allowing students to [stack their state and federal aid](https://opportunityinstitute.org/research/post/college-promise-a-pathway-to-greater-equity-in-opportunity/) to pay for [books, transportation, and living expenses](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf). While [these models are more expensive](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) for states to maintain, they are widely considered more beneficial for [meeting the needs of low-income students](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/).

#### Last-Dollar Programs

By contrast, a last-dollar program calculates how much tuition remains _after_ all other grants—especially Pell Grants—have been applied, and then fills only that remaining gap. Most [statewide promise programs](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) follow this structure because it [stretches limited state dollars further](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf). However, it often provides [little or no additional money](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) to students whose Pell Grants already cover their tuition.

#### A Simple Comparison

Imagine two students: **Student A** qualifies for the maximum Pell Grant, while **Student B** does not qualify for Pell due to higher family income.
-   In a **last-dollar** system, Student A’s Pell Grant likely covers their full tuition, so their [state promise award is effectively zero](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/). Student B, however, receives a [sizable state award](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) to bring their tuition to zero.
-   In a **first-dollar** system, [both students receive the same promise scholarship](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) toward tuition. Student A can then use their [Pell funds for non-tuition costs](https://opportunityinstitute.org/research/post/college-promise-a-pathway-to-greater-equity-in-opportunity/), while Student B relies on other resources for those expenses.

Ultimately, [first-dollar models](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf) deliver more [comprehensive financial relief](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) but require a much [greater public investment](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/).

### Types of State Funding Models

States use several broad approaches to make community college more affordable. These models differ in how visible they are to students and in how directly they reduce tuition bills.

#### Tuition-Free / Promise Programs

Tuition-free or “college promise” programs guarantee that, for eligible students, tuition and sometimes mandatory fees at specified public institutions will be fully covered. While many such programs are [limited to community colleges](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/), some [high-profile local efforts](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/what-are-promise-programs-and-how-can-they-help-make-college-more-affordable) and [equity-focused models](https://opportunityinstitute.org/research/post/college-promise-a-pathway-to-greater-equity-in-opportunity/) extend to certain public four-year universities.

Most promise programs are structured as [last-dollar scholarships](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/), stepping in only after Pell Grants and other aid have been applied. This design helps states control costs, but it can mean that [low-income students](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) receive little extra help if their federal aid already covers tuition. Conversely, [first-dollar models](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf) guarantee a base grant regardless of other aid, which [research suggests](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) has a larger [impact on equity](https://opportunityinstitute.org/research/post/college-promise-a-pathway-to-greater-equity-in-opportunity/)—though these are far more expensive for a state to operate.

#### Typical Features of Promise Programs

While every state differs, these programs generally include specific constraints to manage their budgets:

-   **Residency and Age:** Many programs limit eligibility to [recent high school graduates](https://documents.ncsl.org/wwwncsl/Education/College-Promise-Landscape-v03.pdf) or specific adult populations within the state, as seen in [statewide initiatives like Tennessee Promise](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/).
-   **Geographic Limits:** Students are typically required to enroll at [in-state public institutions](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/what-are-promise-programs-and-how-can-they-help-make-college-more-affordable) within a [defined area](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/).
-   **Academic Standards:** Eligibility often hinges on [maintaining minimum enrollment levels](https://www.collegefortn.org/tennessee-promise-scholarship/) and meeting specific [academic progress standards](https://documents.ncsl.org/wwwncsl/Education/College-Promise-Landscape-v03.pdf).
-   **Time and Credit Caps:** Programs frequently [impose time limits](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf), such as covering only two years of study or a fixed number of credits.
    
Because of these features, promise programs can be highly visible “free college” initiatives while still operating within tight fiscal and eligibility constraints.


#### State Grants and Scholarships

Many states also operate broad grant and scholarship programs that are not branded as “free college” but still significantly reduce community college costs. According to [institutional aid guides](https://frederick.edu/financial-services/state-aid.aspx), these [state-funded programs](https://collegefinance.com/financial-aid/guide-to-need-and-merit-based-financial-aid) often fall into two main categories:

-   **Need-based grants**, which are awarded based on a family’s financial circumstances. These typically [use FAFSA data](https://www.investopedia.com/understanding-scholarships-need-and-merit-4783132) to determine [levels of financial need](https://collegefinance.com/financial-aid/guide-to-need-and-merit-based-financial-aid).
-   **Merit-based scholarships**, which are awarded for [academic performance](https://www.investopedia.com/understanding-scholarships-need-and-merit-4783132) or specific achievements, such as [maintaining a high GPA](https://www.cfwv.com/financial-aid/promise-scholarship/).
    
Need-based state grants are typically designed to bridge the gap between what a family can afford and the total cost of attendance, acting as a crucial complement to [federal programs like the Pell Grant](https://collegefinance.com/financial-aid/guide-to-need-and-merit-based-financial-aid). Merit-based programs, by contrast, focus on [rewarding high-achieving students](https://www.cfwv.com/financial-aid/promise-scholarship/). Research into [college affordability](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org) suggests that because these [merit criteria](https://www.investopedia.com/understanding-scholarships-need-and-merit-4783132) often favor those with stronger prior academic preparation, they can sometimes [shift aid towards middle- or higher-income students](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org).

For community college students, state grants can be applied alongside federal aid to reduce the tuition bill or cover [other educational expenses](https://frederick.edu/financial-services/state-aid.aspx). Unlike many local "promise" programs, these broader [state grants](https://collegefinance.com/financial-aid/guide-to-need-and-merit-based-financial-aid) are often more flexible, allowing students to use them at both two-year and four-year public institutions.

#### Tuition Caps and Low-Cost Systems

Another strategy some states use is to keep community college tuition low across the board rather than offering headline “free college” programs. This can involve setting statewide tuition caps or [providing higher levels of base funding](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) to colleges so they do not need to raise tuition as much. [Federal data on tuition costs](https://nces.ed.gov/fastfacts/display.asp?id=76) indicates that these strategies, alongside [deliberate state policy choices](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org), can keep the barrier to entry low for all residents, while [variation in state tuition averages](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights) remains high across the country.

College Board data on [published tuition and fees](https://research.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/Trends-in-College-Pricing-and-Student-Aid-2024-ADA.pdf) show wide variations in [average in-district community college prices](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/college-pricing/highlights) by state, with some large states maintaining very low listed tuition relative to the national average. In these systems, even without a formal promise program, students may pay [relatively modest tuition](https://educationdata.org/average-cost-of-college) as long as they attend in-district schools. At these institutions, [need-based federal and state grants](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d23/tables/dt23_330.40.asp) can quickly bring a student’s [net tuition cost close to zero](https://research.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/Trends-in-College-Pricing-and-Student-Aid-2024-ADA.pdf).

From a student’s perspective, this model may look less like a special program and more like “community college in this state is just cheaper,” but it still reflects [state-level funding decisions](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) and specific [views on college affordability](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org) that vary by region.

#### Limited or No Statewide Programs

Some states have not adopted prominent statewide promise programs and may offer relatively limited state grant funding targeted to community colleges. In these places, [college affordability](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org) depends on a variety of local factors, but [federal aid play a particularly central role](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/understanding-the-design-of-college-promise-programs-and-where-to-go-from-here/) in making a degree attainable.

For low-income students in such states, the Pell Grant acts as the primary source of tuition support. At community colleges where [annual tuition and fees](https://educationdata.org/average-cost-of-college) are modest, Pell Grants can often [meet the needs](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) of students by covering most or all required costs. Individual colleges may also provide institutional scholarships or emergency aid, though these offerings are [highly specific to each campus](https://collegeaffordability.urban.org) and aren't always visible at the statewide policy level.

From the outside, these states may appear to lack “free college,” yet some students still experience low net tuition because federal and institutional aid fill much of the gap between the sticker price and the [actual cost of attendance](https://educationdata.org/average-cost-of-college). The difference is that the affordability structure is less transparent: instead of a clear guarantee of free tuition, students must [navigate various data systems](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/report-your-data/faq-average-net-price) and piece together support from multiple smaller programs.

### Common Eligibility Requirements Across States

Although every state program has its own rules, there are clear patterns in who is eligible for promise programs and major state aid initiatives. Understanding these patterns helps explain why [“free college” is not automatic](https://documents.ncsl.org/wwwncsl/Education/College-Promise-Landscape-v03.pdf) even where programs exist, as [statewide scholarship designs](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) often include specific gatekeeping mechanisms.

- **Residency and location** Most promise and state grant programs require students to be residents of the state or to have attended high school there. [Specific residency requirements](https://promiseprogramshub.com/free-college-handbook/chapters/18-scholarship-design-who-should-be-eligible/) can be quite strict; for instance, some programs require a minimum period of attendance in a local school district to qualify for [full benefits](https://documents.ncsl.org/wwwncsl/Education/College-Promise-Landscape-v03.pdf).

- **Enrollment status** Many programs require at least half-time or full-time enrollment, often [limiting benefits](https://mhec.maryland.gov/Documents/UPDATED%20PROMISE%20FAQ%209.13.23.pdf) to those who enroll immediately after high school. To stay eligible, students usually need to maintain [continuous enrollment](https://www.montgomerycollege.edu/paying-for-college/financial-aid/types-of-financial-aid/community-college-promise-scholarship.html) and complete a set number of credits each term, a standard [feature of programs like Tennessee Promise](https://www.collegefortn.org/tennessee-promise-scholarship/).

- **Academic progress and GPA** Performance standards are a common hurdle. Programs may specify [minimum high school GPAs](https://promiseprogramshub.com/free-college-handbook/chapters/18-scholarship-design-who-should-be-eligible/) or test scores for entry, and almost all require [maintaining a college GPA](https://mhec.maryland.gov/Documents/UPDATED%20PROMISE%20FAQ%209.13.23.pdf)—often around 2.0 to 2.5—to [keep the award](https://www.collegefortn.org/tennessee-promise-scholarship/), particularly in [merit-based systems](https://www.cfwv.com/financial-aid/promise-scholarship/).

- **Income thresholds and financial need** Many states prioritize students with financial need by incorporating income limits. Some [last-dollar scholarships](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/) restrict eligibility to families whose [Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)](https://www.montgomerycollege.edu/paying-for-college/financial-aid/types-of-financial-aid/community-college-promise-scholarship.html) falls below specific caps, while others use these thresholds to [tier their grant awards](https://frederick.edu/financial-services/state-aid.aspx).

- **Application steps and deadlines** Nearly all programs require the [completion of the FAFSA](https://finaid.org/fafsa/) by a strict deadline. Missing these dates or failing to complete [program-specific acceptance steps](https://www.collegefortn.org/tennessee-promise-scholarship/) can result in a [loss of eligibility](https://frederick.edu/financial-services/state-aid.aspx) for the entire year, regardless of a student's financial situation.

These requirements serve policy goals like encouraging full-time study, but they can also create [administrative burdens](https://journal-bpa.org/index.php/jbpa/article/download/197/114) that [unintentionally screen out](https://promiseprogramshub.com/free-college-handbook/chapters/18-scholarship-design-who-should-be-eligible/) the very students who need the most support.

### How State Programs Interact with Federal Aid

Most state programs are built on top of federal aid rather than replacing it, so understanding the sequence of how aid “stacks” is crucial.

The process usually starts with the [FAFSA](https://finaid.org/fafsa/), which is required for access to federal grants like [Pell](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NmEP38x-1Z8), federal loans, and work-study. Completing the [FAFSA each year](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/completing-the-fafsa) is not only the gateway to federal support but also a core requirement for many [statewide aid initiatives](https://frederick.edu/financial-services/state-aid.aspx) and [promise schemes](https://finaid.org/fafsa/).

For a typical student at a public community college, the stacking of aid often looks like this:

1.  **Federal layer.** After submitting the FAFSA, the student may receive a [Pell Grant](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/pay-for-college/get-help-paying-for-college/scholarships-grants-institutional-aid/what-is-a-pell-grant) based on financial need, plus options for [federal student aid](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2024_National_Profile.pdf) like loans and work-study.
2.  **State layer.** The state applies its [own grants, scholarships, or promise benefits](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/), often using FAFSA data to determine [eligibility and award size](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf).
3.  **Institutional and other aid.** The college may add its own scholarships or emergency grants, which [frequently determine the final net price](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/report-your-data/faq-average-net-price) for the student.
4.  **Final net cost.** The student’s [net price](https://collegecost.ed.gov/net-price) is what remains from the college’s [full cost of attendance](https://collegecost.ed.gov/net-price) after all these grant and scholarship sources have been subtracted.

In [last-dollar promise programs](https://mostpolicyinitiative.org/science-note/statewide-college-promise-programs/), the federal layer strongly shapes how much value students see from state tuition guarantees. Because Pell often covers community college tuition for the lowest-income students, these students may receive [little extra from a last-dollar promise](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/).

In contrast, [first-dollar programs](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) apply the state benefit first, allowing Pell and other grants to be used for [non-tuition expenses](https://opportunityinstitute.org/research/post/college-promise-a-pathway-to-greater-equity-in-opportunity/). This model generally provides more [comprehensive support to Pell-eligible students](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf), though it requires [more significant state investment](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) than the gap-filling last-dollar approach.

### Important Limitations and Trade-Offs

State community college funding programs expand access and reduce costs, but they also come with important limitations and trade-offs that students should understand.

- **Time limits and program length** Many promise and scholarship programs are [limited to two years of study](https://www.ecs.org/wp-content/uploads/State-Information-Request_College-Promise-Programs.pdf) or a credit cap that roughly corresponds to an associate degree. Students who need more time because they attend part-time or change majors may find their [eligibility exhausted](https://promiseprogramshub.com/free-college-handbook/chapters/18-scholarship-design-who-should-be-eligible/) before they complete a credential, a common hurdle in [statewide college promise landscape](https://documents.ncsl.org/wwwncsl/Education/College-Promise-Landscape-v03.pdf) designs.
- **Eligible institutions** Most tuition-free programs apply only to in-state public institutions—specifically [community colleges and technical schools](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/what-are-promise-programs-and-how-can-they-help-make-college-more-affordable). While [some state grants](https://collegefinance.com/financial-aid/guide-to-need-and-merit-based-financial-aid) are portable to [certain private colleges](https://frederick.edu/financial-services/state-aid.aspx), students wishing to attend out-of-state schools typically cannot use these benefits.
- **Part-time students and working adults** A substantial share of community college students work or have family responsibilities, yet many programs require [at least half-time or full-time enrollment](https://mhec.maryland.gov/Documents/UPDATED%20PROMISE%20FAQ%209.13.23.pdf) to qualify. This often [limits the usefulness](https://iwpr.org/making-free-college-programs-work-for-college-students-with-children/) of "free college" for [adult learners and parents](https://promiseprogramshub.com/free-college-handbook/chapters/18-scholarship-design-who-should-be-eligible/) who can only take a few courses at a time.
- **Non-tuition costs** As noted earlier, tuition is only a minority share of the total cost. [Last-dollar models](https://tcf.org/content/report/how-americas-college-promise-would-reshape-the-free-college-landscape/) focused solely on tuition may leave [low-income students](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/do-free-college-programs-really-meet-the-needs-of-low-income-students/) with little additional help once Pell Grants are applied, even though their [biggest financial challenges](https://educationdata.org/average-cost-of-college) involve rent and transportation.
- **Administrative complexity and access** Requirements to complete the FAFSA and meet [separate state deadlines](https://promiseprogramshub.com/free-college-handbook/chapters/18-scholarship-design-who-should-be-eligible/) can create an [administrative burden](https://journal-bpa.org/index.php/jbpa/article/download/197/114) that discourages students. Research indicates that complex renewal rules can [reduce take-up](https://journal-bpa.org/index.php/jbpa/article/download/197/114), particularly among students from [underrepresented backgrounds](https://promiseprogramshub.com/free-college-handbook/chapters/18-scholarship-design-who-should-be-eligible/) who may have less access to professional advising.

These limitations do not negate the value of [statewide tuition-free initiatives](https://feed.georgetown.edu/access-affordability/more-states-offering-free-community-college/), which have been shown to increase enrollment. However, they underscore why families need a clear [mental model](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/what-are-promise-programs-and-how-can-they-help-make-college-more-affordable) of how aid layers interact before relying on a "free" guarantee.

## State-by-State Breakdown

## Alabama

**Program Name(s):**

No major statewide free tuition program for community colleges; limited programs such as the [Alabama Community College Tuition and Fee Waiver](https://www.citizensbank.com/student/articles/states-that-offer-programs-for-free-or-reduced-cost-community-college.aspx) for a single class after GED/Ready to Work completion.

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** Partial; typically [one free college course](https://www.jeffersonstate.edu/support-services/adult-education-ged-preparation/ged-prep-ae-ged/) for eligible GED or Ready to Work completers.
    
-   **Fees:** May vary by institution but are often included in the single-class waiver.
    
-   **Books/materials:** Not covered.
    

**Program Type:**

Last-dollar style tuition waiver for a single class; targeted grant rather than a broad statewide promise program.

**Who Qualifies:**

[Alabama residents](https://www.citizensbank.com/student/articles/states-that-offer-programs-for-free-or-reduced-cost-community-college.aspx) who earn a GED or complete the state [Ready to Work program](https://www.jeffersonstate.edu/support-services/adult-education-ged-preparation/ged-prep-ae-ged/). Other eligibility requirements, such as specific placement scores, vary by college.

**Key Details:**

The waiver generally applies to one course (up to a set credit limit) at participating community colleges. Students must usually meet standard admissions requirements, and while the FAFSA is encouraged for broader aid, it is not always strictly required for this specific waiver.

**Bottom Line:**

Alabama does not offer a universal “free community college” program; instead, it provides [targeted waivers](https://www.jeffersonstate.edu/support-services/adult-education-ged-preparation/ged-prep-ae-ged/) that help GED/Ready to Work completers jumpstart their education with a single starter class.

## Alaska

**Program Name(s):**

[Alaska Education Grant (AEG)](https://acpe.alaska.gov/Funding-Solutions/Alaska-Education-Grant)

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** Partial, via need-based grants [up to $4,000 per year](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/scholarships/alaska-education-grant).
    
-   **Fees:** May be covered indirectly as part of the overall cost of attendance.
    
-   **Books/materials:** Funds apply to educational costs broadly, potentially covering materials if tuition is already met.
    

**Program Type:**

Need-based state grant; [last-dollar style aid](https://www.uaa.alaska.edu/students/financial-aid/types-of-aid/grants.cshtml) layered on top of federal grants.

**Who Qualifies:**

[Alaska residents](https://acpe.alaska.gov/Funding-Solutions/Alaska-Education-Grant) attending qualifying in-state postsecondary institutions, including public two-year colleges. Eligibility is [based on the FAFSA](https://www.petersons.com/scholarship/university-of-alaska-fairbanks-alaska-education-grant-111_277466.aspx), and at least half-time enrollment is required.

**Key Details:**

Awards range from $500 to $4,000 annually. To receive the [maximum award](https://www.petersons.com/scholarship/university-of-alaska-fairbanks-alaska-education-grant-111_277466.aspx), students must often enroll in 15+ credits ("On Time" enrollment). Grants are awarded on a [first-come, first-served basis](https://acpe.alaska.gov/Funding-Solutions/Alaska-Education-Grant) until funds are exhausted.

**Bottom Line:**

Alaska offers a [meaningful need-based grant](https://acpe.alaska.gov/Funding-Solutions/Alaska-Education-Grant) that can substantially reduce community college costs for lower-income students, though it does not guarantee a 100% tuition-free experience for everyone.

## Arizona

**Program Name(s):**

No major statewide tuition-free community college program. The [Arizona Promise Program](https://www.azregents.edu/programs/arizona-promise-program) is primarily for public universities. Some institutional aids, like the [Arizona Promise Scholarship at Pima](https://pima.edu/news/announcements/2025/202510-01-arizona-promise.html), exist.

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** The state-level Arizona Promise Program covers [tuition and mandatory fees at public universities](https://collegereadyaz.com/promise/) after other aid; it does not apply broadly to all community colleges.
    
-   **Fees:** Covered at the university level.
    
-   **Books/materials:** Not covered.
    

**Program Type:**

Last-dollar state scholarship for university students; smaller [institutional scholarships](https://centralaz.edu/pinal-promise/) at specific community colleges.

**Who Qualifies:**

Arizona residents graduating with at least a [2.5 GPA](https://www.azregents.edu/programs/arizona-promise-program) for the university-focused program. Local efforts, like the [Pinal Promise](https://centralaz.edu/pinal-promise/), may require Pinal County residency and community service.

**Key Details:**

State-level promise coverage is [focused on public universities](https://collegereadyaz.com/promise/) (ASU, NAU, U of A). Community college efforts are mostly a [patchwork of county-based programs](https://centralaz.edu/pinal-promise/) with varying rules.

**Bottom Line:**

Arizona affordability relies on federal aid plus local scholarships, as the [statewide promise](https://www.azregents.edu/programs/arizona-promise-program) does not yet extend universally to the community college system.

## Arkansas

**Program Name(s):**

[Arkansas Future Grant (ArFuture)](https://sams.adhe.edu/scholarship/details/ARFUTURE) and [Arkansas Workforce Challenge (AWC)](https://sams.adhe.edu/Scholarship/Details/AWC).

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** Full coverage for [associate and certificate programs](https://www.cccua.edu/pay-for-college/scholarships/state-scholarships-grants.html) in STEM and high-demand fields.
    
-   **Fees:** Full coverage of [mandatory fees](https://sams.adhe.edu/scholarship/details/ARFUTURE).
    
-   **Books/materials:** Not routinely covered.
    

**Program Type:**

[Last-dollar state grant](https://sams.adhe.edu/scholarship/details/ARFUTURE) targeting workforce-focused programs in high-demand sectors.

**Who Qualifies:**

Arkansas high school graduates or residents for [at least 3 years](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/scholarships/arkansas-future-grant-arfuture) without an associate degree. Students must enroll in a designated [STEM or high-demand program](https://sams.adhe.edu/Scholarship/Index?handler=Search&key=Arkansas%20Future%20Grant&categories=&deadlineType=&deadline=&actkey=0&satkey=0&gpakey=0).

**Key Details:**

Recipients must participate in [mentoring and community service](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/scholarships/arkansas-future-grant-arfuture). Crucially, enrollees are expected to [live and work in Arkansas](https://www.atu.edu/ozark/ar-futures.php) for three consecutive years after completing their credential.

**Bottom Line:**

Arkansas offers [generous last-dollar coverage](https://sams.adhe.edu/scholarship/details/ARFUTURE) for residents in workforce-focused community college tracks, provided they commit to staying in the state post-graduation.

## California

**Program Name(s):**

[California College Promise Grant (CCPG)](https://www.cccd.edu/students/financial-aid/californiaprograms/californiacollegepromisegrant.html) and local [California Promise](https://www.cccco.edu/About-Us/Chancellors-Office/Divisions/Educational-Services-and-Support/Student-Service/What-we-do/California-Promise) initiatives.

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** CCPG [waives the per-unit enrollment fees](https://icangotocollege.com/financial-aid/california-college-promise-grant) for eligible students at all CA community colleges.
    
-   **Fees:** Does not cover all mandatory fees; students are usually [responsible for health and parking fees](https://www.cccd.edu/students/financial-aid/californiaprograms/californiacollegepromisegrant.html).
    
-   **Books/materials:** Not covered by the statewide grant.
    

**Program Type:**

[Need-based fee waiver](https://icangotocollege.com/financial-aid/california-college-promise-grant) (first-dollar) for low-income students; last-dollar support for others via local college programs.

**Who Qualifies:**

California residents (and [AB 540–eligible students](https://www.cccd.edu/students/financial-aid/californiaprograms/californiacollegepromisegrant.html)) meeting specific income standards. Students must maintain a [2.0 GPA](https://www.peralta.edu/financial-aid/california-promise) and complete over 50% of attempted coursework to keep the waiver.

**Key Details:**

CCPG serves over [one million students annually](https://www.cccco.edu/About-Us/Chancellors-Office/Divisions/Educational-Services-and-Support/Student-Service/What-we-do/California-Promise). While the waiver is statewide, individual colleges may offer [additional Promise programs](https://www.peralta.edu/financial-aid/california-promise) that help with the first two years of tuition for full-time students.

**Bottom Line:**

California is [exceptionally generous](https://icangotocollege.com/financial-aid/california-college-promise-grant) for low-income residents, effectively making tuition free through a simple fee waiver system.

## Colorado

**Program Name(s):**

[Colorado Promise: Two Free Years of College Expanded](https://cdhe.colorado.gov/students/preparing-for-college/colorado-promise/colorado-promise-ihe-faqs), [Universal High School Scholarship](https://www.bestcolleges.com/news/colorado-bill-free-tuition-two-years/), and [Career Advance Colorado](https://www.bestcolleges.com/news/colorado-bill-free-tuition-two-years/).

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** A [refundable tax credit](https://cdhe.colorado.gov/sites/highered/files/documents/CDHE-Fact%20Sheet-Colorado-Promise.pdf) covering remaining tuition for up to 65 credits after other aid.
    
-   **Fees:** Mandatory fees are covered via the same tax credit mechanism.
    
-   **Books/materials:** Generally not covered.
    

**Program Type:**

Last-dollar coverage delivered as a [state income tax credit](https://cdhe.colorado.gov/sites/highered/files/documents/CDHE-Fact%20Sheet-Colorado-Promise.pdf) (reimbursement).

**Who Qualifies:**

Colorado residents from families with an [Adjusted Gross Income of $90,000 or less](https://cdhe.colorado.gov/students/preparing-for-college/colorado-promise/colorado-promise-ihe-faqs). Students must enroll within [two years of high school completion](https://www.cudenver.com/Blog/Smart-Money/July-2024/Unlocking-Opportunities-Colorado-Promise-Expands-t) and maintain a 2.5 GPA.

**Key Details:**

This is a unique [reimbursement model](https://financialaid.colostate.edu/colorado-promise-two-free-years-of-college-expanded/); students or families receive the funds back when they file state taxes. [Career Advance Colorado](https://www.bestcolleges.com/news/colorado-bill-free-tuition-two-years/) provides more direct, upfront free tuition for specific in-demand fields.

**Bottom Line:**

Colorado provides [expansive last-dollar support](https://cdhe.colorado.gov/students/preparing-for-college/colorado-promise/colorado-promise-ihe-faqs) for middle- and lower-income residents, though students must navigate the tax-credit system to receive the full benefit.

## Connecticut

[Debt-Free Tuition at CT State](https://ctstate.edu/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=22&catid=22) (formerly PACT).

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** Covers the gap [between other grants and community college tuition](https://www.ct.edu/admission/free).
    
-   **Fees:** Mandatory [college services fees](https://catalog.ctstate.edu/content.php?catoid=24&navoid=2934) are included.

**Program Type:**

Last-dollar promise-style program specifically for the [CT State Community College](https://ctstate.edu/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=22&catid=22) system.

[Connecticut residents](https://www.ct.edu/admission/free) who are first-time college students and CT high school graduates. Students must enroll at least [half-time (6+ credits)](https://catalog.ctstate.edu/content.php?catoid=24&navoid=2934) and complete the FAFSA.

**Key Details:**

The program covers [up to 72 earned credit hours](https://ctmirror.org/2026/01/30/ct-pact-community-college-debt-free-tuition-funding/) (roughly two to three years). Awards are [first-come, first-served](https://www.ct.edu/admission/free) based on funding availability.

**Bottom Line:**

Connecticut’s [last-dollar program](https://www.ct.edu/admission/free) makes tuition and fees effectively free for most new in-state community college students who apply early.

## Delaware

[SEED (Student Excellence Equals Degree)](https://www.delawarestudentsuccess.org/seed-and-inspire/) Scholarship.

[Last-dollar statewide promise](https://www.delawarestudentsuccess.org/seed-and-inspire/) for associate degree enrollees.

**Who Qualifies:**

**Bottom Line:**

Delaware’s [SEED program](https://www.delawarestudentsuccess.org/seed-and-inspire/) is one of the nation's most established promise programs, offering a reliable path to a [tuition-free associate degree](https://www.udel.edu/apply/undergraduate-admissions/financing-your-degree/flex-pathway-and-seed/).

## Florida

**Program Name(s):**

[Florida Bright Futures Scholarship Program](https://www.floridabrightfutures.gov/) (Academic Scholars and Medallion Scholars).

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** Florida Academic Scholars (FAS) covers [100% of tuition and fees](https://internationalcollegecounselors.com/florida-bright-futures-scholarship-4/); Medallion Scholars (FMS) covers [75%](https://internationalcollegecounselors.com/florida-bright-futures-scholarship-4/).
    
-   **Fees:** Applicable mandatory fees are included at the same percentage as tuition.
    
-   **Books/materials:** Generally not covered.
    

**Program Type:**

[Merit-based state scholarship](https://www.floridabrightfutures.gov/); functionally a first-dollar award toward tuition and fees.

**Who Qualifies:**

Florida high school graduates meeting strict [GPA and test score](https://scholarships360.org/scholarships/bright-futures-scholarship-requirements/) thresholds. For 2026, FAS requires a [3.5 weighted GPA and a 1330 SAT (or 29 ACT)](https://internationalcollegecounselors.com/florida-bright-futures-scholarship-4/). Service or paid work hours (75–100) are also required.

**Key Details:**

The scholarship is [not based on financial need](https://internationalcollegecounselors.com/florida-bright-futures-scholarship-4/). Students can use it at [public state colleges (community colleges)](https://www.flvs.net/student-parent-resources/college/bright-futures-scholarships) or universities. It allows for a [debt-free graduation](https://www.fastweb.com/student-life/articles/how-i-am-paying-for-college-the-bright-futures-scholarship) for those who maintain the academic requirements.

**Bottom Line:**

Florida relies on [academic merit](https://www.floridabrightfutures.gov/) rather than universal promise, rewarding high-achieving residents with [full or near-full tuition coverage](https://internationalcollegecounselors.com/florida-bright-futures-scholarship-4/).

## Georgia

**Program Name(s):**

[HOPE Grant](https://www.ogeecheetech.edu/financial-aid/hope-career-grant), [HOPE Scholarship](https://finaid.gatech.edu/undergraduate-types-aid/hope-scholarship), and [Zell Miller](https://finaid.gatech.edu/undergraduate-types-aid/hope-scholarship).

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** Covers a [large portion or 100% of standard tuition](https://finaid.gatech.edu/undergraduate-types-aid/hope-scholarship) at technical and public colleges.
    
-   **Fees:** Mandatory fees are often [not fully covered](https://finaid.gatech.edu/undergraduate-types-aid/hope-scholarship).
    
-   **Books/materials:** Not covered.
    

**Program Type:**

[Lottery-funded merit scholarship](https://finaid.gatech.edu/undergraduate-types-aid/hope-scholarship) and workforce grant; functionally first-dollar.

## Wyoming

**Program Name(s):**

[Hathaway Scholarship](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://hathawayscholarships.org/)

**What It Covers:**

-   **Tuition:** Provides [merit-based awards](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://hathawayscholarships.org/) ranging from $840 to $1,680 per semester, which can be used at any Wyoming community college.
    
-   **Fees:** Not fully covered, but scholarship funds can be applied to them.
    
-   **Books/materials:** Not covered.
    

**Program Type:**

Merit-based state scholarship.

**Who Qualifies:**

[Wyoming high school graduates](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://hathawayscholarships.org/) based on their GPA and ACT scores. There are four tiers of awards (Honors, Performance, Opportunity, and Provisional).

**Key Details:**

Even the [lowest tier](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://hathawayscholarships.org/) (Provisional) can cover a significant portion of community college costs. The state also offers a need-based "Hathaway Need" grant for students who already qualify for the merit portion.

**Bottom Line:**

Wyoming uses a [merit-first approach](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://hathawayscholarships.org/). While not "free" for everyone, it makes community college nearly free for any resident who hits basic academic benchmarks in high school.
