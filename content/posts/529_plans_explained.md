## The Most Overlooked Way to Pay for College

A [529 plan](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/saving-college-529-college-savings-plans) is a special kind of investment account that lets you save for education and get valuable tax breaks, especially if you start early and use the money for school costs. When used correctly, [it can leave you with thousands more for college](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/529-plans) than a regular savings account would.

There is a tax-advantaged way to save for education that many families have heard of but don’t really understand: the 529 plan. The confusion usually comes from three places: the money is invested (not just sitting in cash), the [tax rules sound intimidating](https://www.ici.org/pubs/faqs/faqs_529), and each state’s plan works a bit differently.

At its core, a 529 plan is simply a dedicated education account where your contributions are invested and can grow tax-free as long as you later use the money for [qualifying education expenses](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/529-plans-questions-and-answers). In this guide, we’ll walk through what a 529 plan is, how it works step by step, what counts as education expenses, how the tax benefits really work, how it affects financial aid, and when it does and doesn’t make sense.

***

## What Is a 529 Plan?

A [529 plan](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/529-plans) is a tax-advantaged savings plan created under Section 529 of the federal tax code to help families save for education costs. These plans are [sponsored by states, state agencies, or educational institutions](https://www.ici.org/pubs/faqs/faqs_529), but you can usually use the money at eligible schools nationwide, not just in your home state.

Think of it as a “college (and education) investment account” rather than a simple savings account. You put money in, that [money is invested in mutual funds](https://www.morganstanley.com/wealth-investmentsolutions/pdfs/529plan.pdf) or similar portfolios, and it can grow over time—without federal tax on the earnings if used for [qualified education expenses](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/saving-college-529-college-savings-plans).

### Who can open and who is it for?

* The **account owner** can be a [parent, grandparent, relative, or even a non‑relative adult](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/529-plans).
* The **beneficiary** is the person the money is intended for—often a child or student—but [you can even open one for yourself](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/529-plans-questions-and-answers).

**Key takeaway:** A 529 plan is a [flexible investment-style account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/529_plan) designed specifically for education, with built‑in tax advantages when used properly.

***

## How 529 Plans Work

### Step 1: Open an account

You choose a state-sponsored 529 plan (it can be your own state’s plan or another state’s, in most cases) and open an account online or through a financial institution. [All 50 states and D.C. sponsor at least one 529 plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/529_plan).

### Step 2: Contribute money

You deposit money into the account whenever you want—monthly, once a year, or whenever you have extra funds. There’s no federal annual contribution limit, but very large contributions can trigger [gift‑tax rules](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/529-contribution-limits), and states set high overall caps that are [often in the hundreds of thousands of dollars](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/massachusetts-529-plan-contribution-limits) per beneficiary.

### Step 3: Invest the funds

Inside the 529, your money is invested in options you choose from the plan’s menu, like [age‑based portfolios](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/customer-service/529-college-savings-plan-investment-instructions.pdf) (which automatically get more conservative as college approaches) or risk‑based portfolios. The value of the account can go up or down depending on how the investments perform—[there’s no guarantee](https://www.morganstanley.com/wealth-investmentsolutions/pdfs/529plan.pdf).

### Step 4: Let it grow over time

Over the years, contributions plus any investment gains build the account balance. The big benefit is that those [earnings are not taxed each year](https://www.ici.org/pubs/faqs/faqs_529) the way they would be in a normal taxable investment account.

### Step 5: Withdraw for education

When the beneficiary has education expenses, you take withdrawals from the 529 and use them for [qualified education costs](https://www.capitalgroup.com/individual/service-and-support/collegeamerica/distribute/qualified-expenses.html) like tuition, required fees, books, and certain housing and technology costs. As long as the withdrawal matches qualified expenses, the earnings portion comes out [federal tax‑free](https://turbotax.intuit.com/tax-tips/college-and-education/information-on-529-plans/L0vrZiFuC).

## The Tax Advantages

This is where 529 plans really shine.

### Federal tax benefits

1.  **Tax‑free growth**
    Money in a 529 plan [grows without being taxed each year](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/saving-college-529-college-savings-plans) on interest, dividends, or capital gains, unlike a regular brokerage account. This “tax‑deferred” growth lets more of your money stay invested and compounding over time.
2.  **Tax‑free withdrawals for education**
    When you take money out and use it for qualified education expenses, the [earnings are not taxed at the federal level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/529_plan). If you use the money correctly, you never pay federal income tax on the investment growth.

### What counts as “qualified education expenses”?

Rules change over time, but currently common [qualified expenses](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/what-you-can-pay-for-with-a-529-plan) include:

* **Tuition and mandatory fees** at colleges, universities, trade schools, and [many apprenticeship programs](https://meriwether.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/529-Plan-Qualified-Expenses-Updated-Jan-13-2026.pdf).
* **Books, supplies, and required equipment** for courses.
* **Room and board** for college students enrolled at least half‑time, within the school’s [published cost‑of‑attendance limits](https://www.capitalgroup.com/individual/service-and-support/collegeamerica/distribute/qualified-expenses.html) (on‑ or off‑campus).
* **Computers, internet access, and related equipment** used [primarily by the student](https://theeducationplan.com/basics/529-qualified-expenses) while enrolled in eligible postsecondary education.
* Certain **K–12 tuition and related expenses**, up to annual limits, depending on current law.
* Up to a limited amount of **student loan repayment** and qualifying apprenticeship or professional certification programs, [subject to specific caps](https://meriwether.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/529-Plan-Qualified-Expenses-Updated-Jan-13-2026.pdf).

Common things that usually **do not** count as qualified expenses include [transportation, health insurance, and general living expenses](https://www.vertexplanningpartners.com/2019/09/12/529-plans-taking-distributions/) not part of room and board.

## What If You Don’t Use the Money for Education?

A big worry is: “What happens if my child doesn’t go to college or doesn’t use all the money?”

### Non‑qualified withdrawals

If you take money out and **do not** use it for qualified education expenses:

- Your original contributions come back **tax‑free**, since you [put them in after tax](https://www.savingforcollege.com/intro-to-529s/what-is-the-penalty-on-an-unused-529-plan).
- The **earnings portion** of the withdrawal is generally subject to federal income tax plus a [10% federal penalty](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-avoid-the-10-percent-tax-penalty-on-non-qualified-distributions).

Many states will also [“recapture” any state deductions or credits](https://www.mefa.org/article/the-massachusetts-college-savings-tax-deduction/) you claimed on the original contributions if you later make non‑qualified withdrawals.

### Important exceptions to the 10% penalty

The 10% penalty on earnings is **waived** (though regular income tax on earnings may still apply) in [several situations](https://www.collegeadvantage.com/blog/blog-detail/posts/2023/10/10/unique-non-qualified-withdrawals-lead-to-unique-exceptions), including:

- The beneficiary gets a **tax‑free scholarship** (you can withdraw up to the scholarship amount penalty‑free).
- The beneficiary **attends a U.S. military academy**.
- The beneficiary **dies or becomes disabled**.
- Certain cases where [education tax credits](https://turbotax.intuit.com/tax-tips/college-and-education/information-on-529-plans/L0vrZiFuC) are claimed on the same expenses.


### Changing the beneficiary

If one child does not use the money, you can usually [change the beneficiary to another qualifying family member](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/529-plans-questions-and-answers)—such as a sibling, cousin, parent, or even a future grandchild. This lets the money stay in the 529 and preserves the tax benefits for another student. Additionally, under recent rules, you may even be able to [roll over unused funds into a Roth IRA](https://www.ameriprise.com/financial-goals-priorities/education-planning/new-529-plan-rules) for the beneficiary, subject to certain lifetime limits and eligibility requirements.

**Key insight:** 529 plans are [more flexible than many people think](https://www.savingforcollege.com/intro-to-529s/what-is-the-penalty-on-an-unused-529-plan); while there are penalties for using money on non‑education costs, you have multiple options before you ever reach that point.

## Types of 529 Plans

There are two main types of 529 plans, and most families use the first type.

### College savings plans (most common)

- Work like an investment account: you choose [investment options](https://www.fidelity.com/529-plans/investment-options), and the balance rises or falls with market performance.
- Can be used at a [wide range of eligible colleges](https://www.schwab.com/529-plan), universities, trade schools, and some K–12 and apprenticeship settings.


### Prepaid tuition plans

- Let you **lock in tuition** at [today’s rates](https://www.collegewell.com/private-college-529-plan/) for future study at specific colleges or within a state system.
- Usually more limited: often apply only to in‑state public schools or a defined group of institutions, and [may not cover room, board, or other expenses](https://boulaygroup.com/choosing-a-529-plan-prepaid-tuition-vs-college-savings-plans/).

**Key takeaway:** Most families choose [investment‑based 529 college savings plans](https://www.ici.org/pubs/faqs/faqs_529) because they are more flexible and widely available than prepaid tuition plans.

## How 529 Plans Affect Financial Aid

One of the most common myths is that having a 529 plan will disqualify your child from receiving financial aid. In reality, [529 plans are treated very favorably](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/yes-your-529-plan-will-affect-financial-aid) in the federal financial aid formula.

### The FAFSA and the Student Aid Index (SAI)

When you fill out the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA), the government calculates your **Student Aid Index (SAI)**. Here is how different 529 accounts impact that number:

- **Parent-owned accounts:** If a parent owns the 529 plan, it is considered a parental asset. Only a maximum of [5.64% of the account’s value](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/education-college-savings/financial-aid) is counted toward the SAI. This means a $10,000 savings balance might only reduce your aid eligibility by about $564.
- **Student-owned accounts:** Interestingly, the FAFSA treats 529 plans owned by a dependent student [as a parental asset](https://www.saxonfinancialgroup.com/how-529-plans-affect-fafsa/) rather than a student asset. This is a huge benefit, as other student assets (like a standard savings account) are typically assessed at a much higher 20% rate.
- **Grandparent-owned accounts:** Under the latest [FAFSA simplification rules](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/new-fafsa-removes-roadblocks-for-grandparent-529-plans), accounts owned by grandparents or other relatives are **not reported as assets** on the FAFSA at all. Furthermore, when the money is spent on the student's education, those distributions are no longer counted as student income.

### CSS Profile Schools

Be aware that some private colleges use a different form called the **CSS Profile** to award their own institutional aid. These schools may [look more closely at all family assets](https://www.cirkledin.com/library/scholarships-and-financial-aid/529-affect-fafsa-css-profile-financial-aid/), including accounts owned by non-custodial parents or other relatives.

**Key takeaway:** While a 529 plan does count as an asset, its impact on need-based aid is usually minimal compared to the [enormous benefit of having the funds available](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/education-college-savings/financial-aid) to pay for school.

## Pros and Cons of 529 Plans

Here’s a simple side‑by‑side view.

| Aspect | Pros | Cons |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Taxes** | [Tax‑free growth and withdrawals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/529_plan) for qualified expenses. | Earnings on non‑qualified withdrawals are taxed and usually face a [10% penalty](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-avoid-the-10-percent-tax-penalty-on-non-qualified-distributions). |
| **Flexibility** | Can be used at [many colleges, trade schools, and some K–12](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/529-plans); beneficiary can be changed to another family member. | Using funds for non‑education purposes loses tax benefits and may trigger [penalties and state “clawbacks.”](https://www.savingforcollege.com/intro-to-529s/what-is-the-penalty-on-an-unused-529-plan) |
| **Limits** | Very [high total contribution limits](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/massachusetts-529-plan-contribution-limits) (often hundreds of thousands per beneficiary). | Very large contributions may run into [gift‑tax considerations](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/529-contribution-limits). |
| **Investment** | Professionally managed, easy one‑stop portfolios like [age‑based options](https://www.fidelity.com/bin-public/060_www_fidelity_com/documents/customer-service/529-college-savings-plan-investment-instructions.pdf). | Investments can lose value; [no guarantee](https://www.morganstanley.com/wealth-investmentsolutions/pdfs/529plan.pdf) you’ll have enough for college. |
| **State benefits** | Many states offer [deductions or credits](https://educationdata.org/529-tax-deductions-by-state) for contributions. | Some states offer [no tax break](https://www.ici.org/pubs/faqs/faqs_529), or only for their own in‑state plan. |

**Key takeaway:** 529 plans are powerful for [tax‑efficient education saving](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/saving-college-529-college-savings-plans), but you should be comfortable with market risk and the idea that the money is best used for education.

## How 529 Plans Affect Financial Aid

Families often worry that saving in a 529 will “kill” their aid eligibility. In reality, 529s are usually [one of the most financial‑aid‑friendly ways to save](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/yes-your-529-plan-will-affect-financial-aid).

### How the FAFSA treats 529 accounts

On the [Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA)](https://studentaid.gov/sites/default/files/2026-27-fafsa-form.pdf):

- **Parent‑owned 529s for a dependent student** are treated as [parent assets](https://www.saxonfinancialgroup.com/how-529-plans-affect-fafsa/), not student assets.
- Parent assets are counted at a maximum of about **5.64%** of their value in the aid formula, which is [much more favorable than student assets](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/education-college-savings/financial-aid) (which can be assessed at 20%).
- For example, a 10,000‑dollar 529 might reduce aid eligibility by [roughly 564 dollars](https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/education-college-savings/financial-aid), whereas a 10,000‑dollar asset owned directly by the student could reduce aid by around 2,000 dollars.

Qualified withdrawals from a parent‑ or student‑owned 529 to pay current‑year college costs are **not counted as income** on the FAFSA, so they [don’t reduce future aid](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/yes-your-529-plan-will-affect-financial-aid) the way some other funding sources can.

### Grandparent‑ or other relative‑owned 529s

Under [current FAFSA rules for 2026](https://www.kiplinger.com/personal-finance/college/use-the-529-grandparent-loophole-to-maximize-college-savings), accounts owned by grandparents or other relatives are **not reported as assets** at all. Furthermore, [distributions from these accounts](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/grandparent-owned-529s-get-boost) no longer count as student income, effectively removing the "financial aid trap" that previously penalized these gifts. However, these accounts may still be [considered by schools using the CSS Profile](https://www.cirkledin.com/library/scholarships-and-financial-aid/529-affect-fafsa-css-profile-financial-aid/) for institutional aid.

**Key insight:** Compared with many other ways of holding money for a child, [529 plans usually have a relatively modest impact](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/yes-your-529-plan-will-affect-financial-aid) on need‑based financial aid.

## When a 529 Plan Makes Sense

### Good fit for many families when:

- You’re planning **years ahead** for education costs rather than just the next semester, allowing [investments time to grow](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/529-plans).
- You want to invest, not just save in cash, and are comfortable with [market ups and downs](https://www.morganstanley.com/wealth-investmentsolutions/pdfs/529plan.pdf) over time.
- You like the idea of **tax‑free growth and withdrawals** for education, and possibly [state tax deductions or credits](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/massachusetts-529-plan-contribution-limits).
- You expect that someone in the family—child, sibling, or even you—will use the money for [education at some point](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/529-plans-questions-and-answers).
- You are interested in the new [2026 expansion of K–12 benefits](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/529-eligible-expenses-what-can-529-be-used), which now allows up to $20,000 per year for tuition, tutoring, and curriculum.


### Less ideal when:

- You’re saving for **very short‑term needs**, like tuition due in a year or two, where [market swings could be risky](https://www.morganstanley.com/wealth-investmentsolutions/pdfs/529plan.pdf).
- You’re very unsure whether the beneficiary or any family member will use the funds for education and strongly dislike the idea of any [penalties](https://www.savingforcollege.com/intro-to-529s/what-is-the-penalty-on-an-unused-529-plan).
- You need **complete flexibility** to use the money for any purpose at any time without any [tax strings attached](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/saving-college-529-college-savings-plans) (in which case, a regular savings or brokerage account might be better).

## Practical “How It Works” Example

Imagine you start when your child is a baby and put 200 dollars a month into a 529 for 18 years.

* You would contribute about 43,200 dollars total (200 × 12 × 18).
* If the investments earned an average of around 6% per year, you might end up with [roughly 75,000–80,000 dollars by college time](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/529-plans) (this is just a simple illustration, not a guarantee).

In a [regular taxable investment account](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/saving-college-529-college-savings-plans), you could owe income or capital gains tax on many of those earnings over the years and when you sell. In a 529, if you use that 75,000–80,000 dollars for [qualified education expenses](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/529-plans-questions-and-answers), the **entire gain portion can be federal tax‑free**, potentially leaving several thousand dollars more for school than a taxable account would.

Because this is a simplified example and [returns are never guaranteed](https://www.morganstanley.com/wealth-investmentsolutions/pdfs/529plan.pdf), it’s wise to think of it as showing the **direction** of the benefit: the longer the money has to grow, the more valuable the 529’s tax break can become.

## Common Misconceptions About 529 Plans

Let’s clear up some of the biggest myths.

### “You must use your own state’s plan.”

Not true. Most states allow you to invest in **any** state’s 529 savings plan, and you can still [use the money at eligible schools nationwide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/529-plans). However, some states only give their [state tax deduction or credit](https://educationdata.org/529-tax-deductions-by-state) if you use that state’s own plan, so you should always [check your home state’s rules](https://www.ici.org/pubs/faqs/faqs_529).

### “The money is locked up forever.”

You can always withdraw from a 529 plan; the question is whether the withdrawal is qualified or non‑qualified. If it’s non‑qualified, you’ll owe tax and [possibly a 10% penalty on earnings](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-avoid-the-10-percent-tax-penalty-on-non-qualified-distributions), but your [contributions are never penalized](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/529-plans-questions-and-answers), and there are exceptions and options like [changing the beneficiary](https://www.savingforcollege.com/intro-to-529s/what-is-the-penalty-on-an-unused-529-plan).

### “It only covers tuition.”

529 funds can be used for [much more than just tuition](https://www.capitalgroup.com/individual/service-and-support/collegeamerica/distribute/qualified-expenses.html): eligible uses include fees, books, supplies, [room and board for college](https://www.vertexplanningpartners.com/2019/09/12/529-plans-taking-distributions/), certain computers and internet costs, some K–12 expenses, and [certain loan and training costs](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/what-you-can-pay-for-with-a-529-plan). Transportation, health insurance, and most extracurricular fees usually do **not** qualify, so it’s important to match withdrawals to actual qualified expenses.

### “It’s only for wealthy families.”

In reality, only a small share of families have 529 accounts, and many contributions are relatively modest. You can [start with small amounts](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/saving-college-529-college-savings-plans), add to the account over time, and still benefit from [tax‑free growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/529_plan) and possibly [state deductions](https://www.ici.org/pubs/faqs/faqs_529) on those contributions.

## A Powerful Tool If You Understand It

A 529 plan is one of the most effective and flexible tools available for saving and investing for education in a tax‑smart way. By combining long‑term investing with [tax‑free growth and withdrawals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/529_plan) for qualified expenses—and in many cases [state tax breaks](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/massachusetts-529-plan-contribution-limits) as well—it can significantly boost how far your education dollars go over time.

As of 2026, these plans are even more versatile; the [annual K–12 withdrawal limit has doubled to $20,000](https://www.chase.com/personal/investments/learning-and-insights/article/new-529-plan-rules-2026), and funds can now be used for a broader range of expenses like [tutoring and standardized test fees](https://www.smart529.com/resources/expanded-529-plan-benefits-under-the-one-big-beautiful-bill-act.html). If you understand the basics—what counts as a [qualified expense](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/what-you-can-pay-for-with-a-529-plan), how taxes and penalties work, and how it [affects financial aid](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/yes-your-529-plan-will-affect-financial-aid)—you can use a 529 with confidence as a cornerstone of your family's financial future.
