## Key Points

*   You cannot receive federal grants, federal student loans, or federal work‑study as an [undocumented or DACA student](https://studentaid.gov/sites/default/files/financial-aid-and-undocumented-students.pdf).
*   You may still qualify for state financial aid in certain states (often through a [state “Dream Act” or separate state aid form](https://understandingfafsa.org/undocumented-students/), not the FAFSA).
*   Many states allow undocumented students to pay [in‑state tuition](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/government/politics-elections/2025/07/18/4-things-know-about-state-tuition-noncitizens) at public colleges if they meet residency and high school attendance requirements.
*   Some colleges—especially private and well‑resourced schools—offer substantial [institutional grants and scholarships](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2016/09/14/colleges-that-accept-undocumented-students-as-domestic-students/) to undocumented students, sometimes meeting full demonstrated need.
*   [Private scholarships](https://www.thedream.us/scholarships/) (like TheDream.US and many local awards) do not always require U.S. citizenship and can cover large portions of your costs.
*   [State policy and location](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/) matter more for undocumented students than for most other students; where you live and where you enroll can greatly change what you pay.
*   DACA status does not unlock federal aid, but it can provide [work authorization](https://www.thedream.us/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Career-Services-Practioners-Fact-Sheet-March2022.pdf) and, in some states, better access to in‑state tuition and state programs.
*   Colleges may treat undocumented students as [domestic or international applicants](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/); this choice can dramatically change both admissions odds and financial aid.
*   You should use each college’s [Net Price Calculator](https://www.naicu.edu/research/federal-tool-guide/net-price-calculator/) (NPC) to estimate cost and then layer in state aid and private scholarships to build a realistic plan.
*   Success usually comes from combining in‑state tuition, state and institutional aid, scholarships, and work income—not from one [single source](https://www.nilc.org/resources/toolkit-higher-education-for-immigrant-students/).

***

Many undocumented students, DACA recipients, and mixed‑status families have been told, “You can’t get financial aid, so college isn’t possible.” This idea is powerful—and often [wrong or at least incomplete](https://www.appily.com/guidance/articles/first-gen-underrepresented-students/undocumented-student-aid-eligibility).

It is true that undocumented and DACA students are **not eligible for federal student aid**, including Pell Grants and [federal student loans](https://studentaid.gov/sites/default/files/financial-aid-and-undocumented-students.pdf). But federal aid is only one part of the financial aid system; many states, colleges, and private organizations have created [alternative pathways](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/) that do not depend on citizenship.

This article focuses on what **is** possible: state aid, [in‑state tuition](https://thebestschools.org/resources/undocumented-students/financial-aid/), institutional support, and private scholarships, plus a step‑by‑step strategy to [make college affordable](https://www.collegevaluesonline.com/resources/financial-aid/financial-aid-undocumented-students/) even without federal aid.

## What Undocumented Students Are NOT Eligible For

### Federal Financial Aid Programs

Under current federal rules, undocumented students—including DACA recipients—are **not eligible for federal student aid**. That means you cannot receive:

*   Federal Pell Grants
*   Federal Supplemental Educational Opportunity Grants (FSEOG)
*   [Federal Direct (Stafford) Loans or PLUS Loans](https://studentaid.gov/sites/default/files/financial-aid-and-undocumented-students.pdf)
*   Federal Work‑Study funding

The U.S. Department of Education’s own [guidance](https://financialaidtoolkit.ed.gov/tk/outreach/target/noncitizens.jsp) confirms that undocumented and DACA students cannot receive federal student aid, even if they have a Social Security Number (SSN).

### FAFSA and Why It Still Comes Up

The Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) is the federal form used to determine eligibility for federal grants, loans, and work‑study. Since undocumented students are not eligible for federal aid, the FAFSA **cannot award them federal money**, although some [colleges or states](https://understandingfafsa.org/undocumented-students/) may use FAFSA data for their own purposes.

DACA recipients with SSNs sometimes complete the FAFSA if a college or state asks for it to determine **institutional** or **state** aid, but they still will not receive federal grants or loans. For most undocumented students without an SSN, the FAFSA is not used at all for federal aid, though some [schools may ask](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/) for a *paper* version or an institutional form instead.

### Why This Restriction Exists

Federal law limits student aid to U.S. citizens and certain categories of [“eligible noncitizens”](https://financialaidtoolkit.ed.gov/tk/outreach/target/noncitizens.jsp) (such as permanent residents and refugees), which does **not** include undocumented students or most DACA recipients. These rules are built into the [Higher Education Act](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c34cdda94c282ae667d4f59ece5289905b5de7dd) and related federal regulations, and financial aid offices are legally required to follow them.

## What Undocumented Students CAN Access

Most undocumented students who make college work do it by **combining several non‑federal sources of aid**. Below are the main categories you can realistically pursue.

### a. State Financial Aid Programs

Some states have created their own systems to provide grants or scholarships to undocumented students, separate from federal aid. Common patterns include:

*   States that offer **state grants** or scholarships to all students who meet residency and high school requirements, regardless of immigration status.
*   States that have a [“Dream Act”‑style law](https://understandingfafsa.org/undocumented-students/) allowing undocumented students to apply for state aid through a **state‑specific application** (not FAFSA).

Examples of supportive models include:

*   **California**: The [California Dream Act Application (CADAA)](https://www.berkeleycitycollege.edu/ucrc/resources-2/california-dream-act-ab-540-information/) allows eligible undocumented and AB 540/AB 2000/SB 68 students to receive state grants like Cal Grants, UC Grants, State University Grants, and other institutional awards.
*   **States listed by Immigrants Rising**: States such as California, Colorado, Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Oregon, Texas, Utah, Washington, and several others provide some combination of [state financial aid or institutional scholarships](https://www.presidentsalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Higher-Ed-Guide-to-Tuition-Financial-Aid-Other-Funding-for-Undocumented-Students-September-2020.pdf) to undocumented students who meet residency rules.

At the same time, some states **do not** offer state aid to undocumented students and may even bar them from enrolling in certain public institutions. Because these laws change, you must always check up‑to‑date information for your specific state, often through a state higher‑education agency or the [Higher Ed Immigration Portal](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/).

Key implications for you:

*   Look up state rules early—your eligibility for state aid can depend on how long you’ve lived there and whether you [attended high school](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/government/politics-elections/2025/07/18/4-things-know-about-state-tuition-noncitizens) in the state.
*   Expect a **separate application** (for example, a state Dream Act form) instead of or in addition to the FAFSA.
*   Deadlines for state applications can be earlier than college application deadlines; [missing them](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2025-26_Tip_Sheet_Undocumented_Students.pdf) can cost you thousands of dollars.

### b. In‑State Tuition Benefits

In‑state tuition is often a much bigger financial benefit than any single scholarship because it dramatically lowers the basic [cost of attendance](https://sheeo.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Undocumented-Tuition-and-Fee-Survey.pdf) at public colleges.

Across the country:

*   Roughly twenty‑plus states and the District of Columbia allow undocumented students, including many DACA recipients, to pay [in‑state tuition](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/04/11/battles-brew-over-in-state-tuition-for-undocumented-students.html) at public colleges if they meet certain requirements.
*   Requirements usually involve having **attended and graduated from a high school in the state** and sometimes living there for a certain number of years.
*   A smaller group of states [restricts in‑state tuition](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/) or explicitly bars undocumented students from some or all public institutions.

What this means for you:

*   Research whether your state offers in‑state rates to undocumented students and what you must do (years in state, high school attendance, affidavits, etc.) to [qualify](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/government/politics-elections/2025/07/18/4-things-know-about-state-tuition-noncitizens).
*   If your home state is restrictive, consider applying to colleges in more [supportive states](https://www.naspa.org/blog/undocumented-students-and-related-policies-in-region-2-states) where you might still qualify for in‑state tuition after meeting residency rules or through special agreements.

### c. Institutional Aid From Colleges

“Institutional aid” is money that comes directly from the college, not from the federal or state government. It can include:

*   Need‑based grants or scholarships
*   Merit scholarships (for grades, test scores, talent, leadership)
*   Institutional work programs or institutional loans

Colleges—especially private colleges—have much more flexibility than the federal government in deciding whether to award aid to undocumented students. Some [important patterns](https://counselors.collegeboard.org/financial-aid/undocumented-students):

*   Many **private colleges** consider undocumented students as [“domestic” applicants](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2016/09/14/colleges-that-accept-undocumented-students-as-domestic-students/), allowing them to compete for the same institutional aid as U.S. citizens.
*   A number of selective private colleges explicitly commit to meeting [100% of demonstrated financial need](https://www.appily.com/guidance/articles/first-gen-underrepresented-students/undocumented-student-aid-eligibility) for admitted undocumented students, with or without DACA.
*   Other colleges classify undocumented students as **international students**, which often means higher sticker prices and more limited financial aid.

Colleges that support undocumented students may ask you to:

*   Complete an **institutional financial aid form**, the CSS Profile, or a [paper FAFSA](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/) sent directly to the college (not to the federal government).
*   Provide family financial information similar to what U.S. citizens submit, even though you are not eligible for federal aid.

Because policies vary widely, you should **contact each college’s financial aid office** and ask specifically how they treat undocumented and DACA students.

### d. Private Scholarships

Private scholarships come from organizations outside government and colleges: nonprofits, community foundations, employers, advocacy groups, and more. These scholarships can be especially important for undocumented students because they often [do not require U.S. citizenship](https://getschooled.com/article/1320-scholarships-for-undocumented-daca-mented-students-get-that-money).

Examples and patterns:

*   **National scholarships for undocumented students**, such as [TheDream.US](https://www.thedream.us/scholarships/), offer substantial awards—sometimes tens of thousands of dollars or full funding at partner colleges—for undocumented students with or without DACA or TPS.
*   Many regional and local scholarships (community foundations, local businesses, cultural and civic organizations) explicitly state that undocumented students or DACA recipients are [eligible](https://www.appily.com/guidance/articles/first-gen-underrepresented-students/undocumented-student-aid-eligibility).
*   Some private scholarships are neutral on immigration status but may require a Social Security Number for tax or payment reasons; others use [alternate methods](https://counselors.collegeboard.org/financial-aid/undocumented-students) to disburse funds.

When reviewing scholarships, look carefully for phrases like **“open to undocumented students”**, **“DACA recipients eligible”**, or **“no citizenship required.”**

## The Importance of State Policy

Because federal aid is off the table, **state policy becomes the foundation** of what is financially possible for undocumented students.

Key reasons state policy matters so much:

*   **In‑state tuition**: Whether you pay in‑state, out‑of‑state, or international rates at public colleges is generally decided by [state law or statewide boards](https://sheeo.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Undocumented-Tuition-and-Fee-Survey.pdf).
*   **State grants and scholarships**: Only some states open their grant programs to undocumented students, often via [special state applications](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/).
*   **Enrollment limits**: A handful of states [restrict or bar](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/) undocumented students from enrolling at certain public institutions at all.

Organizations tracking state policies report that more than twenty states have inclusive “tuition equity” policies, while others remain restrictive or prohibitive. For undocumented students, this means that [moving or choosing to study in a different state](https://www.naspa.org/blog/undocumented-students-and-related-policies-in-region-2-states) can dramatically change both eligibility and cost.

You can usually learn your state’s rules by:

*   Visiting the [Higher Ed Immigration Portal](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/) or similar state‑policy trackers.
*   Checking your state’s higher education agency website.
*   Asking [trusted counselors or community organizations](https://www.nilc.org/resources/toolkit-higher-education-for-immigrant-students/) familiar with undocumented student issues.

## DACA vs. Undocumented: What’s the Difference for Aid?

### What DACA Does and Does Not Do

Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) provides temporary protection from deportation and, crucially, **work authorization** for eligible recipients. With a valid Employment Authorization Document (EAD), DACA recipients can [legally work and earn income](https://www.justia.com/immigration/daca/daca-and-employment/)—often at higher wages than informal or cash‑only jobs.

However, DACA **does not make you eligible for federal financial aid**. Federal Student Aid [guidance](https://financialaidtoolkit.ed.gov/tk/outreach/target/noncitizens.jsp) explicitly states that DACA students are not eligible for federal grants, loans, or work‑study, even if they have an SSN and file a FAFSA.

### Practical Differences for Paying for College

In practice, DACA status can affect financial aid in several ways:

*   **Employment and income**: DACA holders can [legally work on or off campus](https://www.thedream.us/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Career-Services-Practioners-Fact-Sheet-March2022.pdf) when employers follow federal employment verification rules, which can make it easier to contribute to tuition and living costs.
*   **State‑level benefits**: Some states explicitly extend certain benefits—like in‑state tuition or specific state aid programs—to [DACA recipients](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/), even when undocumented students without DACA may not be eligible.
*   **Institutional policies**: Some colleges have slightly different procedures or [opportunities for DACA students](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2025/10/15/how-institutions-can-help-address-undocumented-college-students-basic-needs/) versus undocumented students without DACA, especially for campus jobs or internships.

Still, when it comes to **formal financial aid**, the **differences are often smaller than expected**. Both DACA and non‑DACA undocumented students are excluded from federal aid and depend heavily on state policy, institutional aid, and [private scholarships](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/).

## How Colleges Treat Undocumented Students

Colleges have significant discretion in how they [categorize undocumented students](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ba14660e6c6fd1f2410d17469b06725b21fd4baa), and this choice can strongly affect cost and access to aid.

### Domestic vs. International Classification

Many colleges historically treated undocumented students as **international applicants**, especially in admissions and financial aid. This can have several consequences:

*   Higher tuition rates (international or out‑of‑state pricing)
*   Admission decisions made in a [smaller and often more competitive](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/) international pool
*   Limited or no access to institutional need‑based aid reserved for domestic students

In response, a growing number of private colleges have shifted to treating undocumented students—sometimes including DACA recipients—as **domestic** applicants. When that happens, undocumented students may:

*   Compete in the **domestic admissions pool**
*   Qualify for [need‑based institutional aid](https://www.nilc.org/resources/toolkit-higher-education-for-immigrant-students/) similar to other domestic students
*   Be considered for merit scholarships that are open to all domestic applicants

Resources tracking these shifts list dozens of private colleges that [openly accept and fund](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2016/09/14/colleges-that-accept-undocumented-students-as-domestic-students/) undocumented students as domestic students, including some that promise to meet [full demonstrated need](https://www.appily.com/guidance/articles/first-gen-underrepresented-students/undocumented-student-aid-eligibility).

### Why This Matters to You

When researching colleges, you should always ask:

*   “Do you treat undocumented (and/or DACA) students as domestic or international applicants for admissions and financial aid?”
*   “What institutional aid is available specifically to undocumented or DACA students?”

The difference between being treated as domestic vs. international can easily equal **tens of thousands of dollars per year** in cost.

## Real‑World Scenarios

These examples illustrate how different combinations of state, institutional, and private aid can make college affordable.

### Scenario 1: Student in a Supportive State Using State Aid

Ana is an undocumented student who has lived in her state for most of high school and graduates from a local public high school. Her state allows undocumented students to pay in‑state tuition and apply for a state grant through a [state Dream Act application](https://understandingfafsa.org/undocumented-students/).

Ana:

*   Qualifies for **in‑state tuition** at a public university, cutting her tuition dramatically.
*   Submits the [California Dream Act Application](https://www.berkeleycitycollege.edu/ucrc/resources-2/california-dream-act-ab-540-information/) (or her state's equivalent) on time and receives a need‑based state grant.
*   Applies to several public campuses and chooses one that also offers a small **institutional scholarship** for high‑achieving in‑state students.

By stacking in‑state tuition, [state grants](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/), and a modest institutional scholarship, Ana reduces her net cost to an amount she can cover with part‑time work and family contributions.

### Scenario 2: Student Relying on Private Scholarships

Luis lives in a restrictive state that does not offer in‑state tuition or state aid to undocumented students, and his local public universities charge him [out‑of‑state or international tuition rates](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/).

Luis:

*   Applies broadly to national and regional **private scholarships** specifically open to undocumented and DACA students.
*   Wins a major scholarship from a national organization like [TheDream.US](https://www.thedream.us/scholarships/), which partners with a public university in a more supportive state.
*   Relocates to attend a [partner college](https://www.nilc.org/resources/toolkit-higher-education-for-immigrant-students/) where the scholarship covers a large share of tuition, fees, and possibly housing for his bachelor’s degree.

Even though his home state is not supportive, Luis leverages private funding plus a more [inclusive out‑of‑state college](https://www.naspa.org/blog/undocumented-students-and-related-policies-in-region-2-states) to make his degree possible.

### Scenario 3: Student at a College With Strong Institutional Aid

Marisol is an undocumented student with strong grades and test scores who applies to several private colleges that treat [undocumented students as domestic applicants](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2016/09/14/colleges-that-accept-undocumented-students-as-domestic-students/).

Marisol:

*   Is admitted to a private college that [promises to meet 100% of demonstrated financial need](https://www.appily.com/guidance/articles/first-gen-underrepresented-students/undocumented-student-aid-eligibility) for all domestic students, including undocumented students.
*   Submits required financial aid forms (like the CSS Profile and an [institutional aid application](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/)).
*   Receives a package made up entirely of **institutional grants and scholarships**, plus campus work not funded by federal work‑study.

Marisol’s out‑of‑pocket cost ends up similar to or lower than attending her in‑state public university at international tuition rates.

## Building a Financial Strategy as an Undocumented Student

Think of paying for college as building a **plan**, not filling out one form. Here is a practical sequence of steps.

### a. Focus on Supportive States

1.  **Research your current state’s policies** on:
    *   In‑state tuition for undocumented and/or DACA students
    *   Eligibility for state grants or scholarships
    *   Any [restrictions on enrollment](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/) at public institutions.
2.  If your state is highly restrictive, consider:
    *   Applying to colleges in **more inclusive states** where [in‑state tuition and some state aid](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/) may be available after meeting residency criteria.
    *   Looking at colleges that partner with national scholarships like [TheDream.US](https://www.thedream.us/scholarships/) in supportive states.

### b. Research College‑Specific Policies

For each college on your list:

*   Check the admissions and financial aid pages for [“undocumented”, “DACA”, or “Dreamer”](https://counselors.collegeboard.org/financial-aid/undocumented-students) information.
*   Contact the financial aid office (you can have a counselor or trusted adult call if you are uncomfortable):
    *   Ask whether the college treats [undocumented students as domestic or international](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/).
    *   Ask what forms undocumented students must submit to be considered for **institutional aid**.
    *   Confirm whether undocumented students can access campus jobs funded from [institutional (not federal) money](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2025-26_Tip_Sheet_Undocumented_Students.pdf).

### c. Apply Broadly for Scholarships

Build a scholarship strategy with layers:

*   **National programs** specifically for undocumented or DACA students (e.g., national Dreamer scholarships).
*   **Regional and local awards** from community foundations, cultural organizations, and local businesses.
*   **College‑specific scholarships**—some colleges have additional internal scholarships you must apply for separately.

Start early, keep a spreadsheet of deadlines and requirements, and do not limit yourself to a few [“big name” scholarships](https://getschooled.com/article/1320-scholarships-for-undocumented-daca-mented-students-get-that-money).

### d. Prioritize Affordable Schools

When making your college list, include:

*   At least **two to three financial “safety” schools** where you are confident you can afford costs through a mix of in‑state tuition, probable aid, and realistic work income.
*   **Supportive public universities** in states with inclusive policies, if available.
*   [Private colleges with strong institutional aid](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2016/09/14/colleges-that-accept-undocumented-students-as-domestic-students/) and explicit commitments to undocumented students.

Avoid building a list composed only of expensive private or out‑of‑state public colleges that do not [clearly support undocumented students](https://www.nilc.org/resources/toolkit-higher-education-for-immigrant-students/).

### e. Use Net Price Calculators (NPCs)

Every college that participates in federal aid programs is required by law to provide a [Net Price Calculator](https://www.naicu.edu/research/federal-tool-guide/net-price-calculator/) on its website.

*   NPCs estimate what [students like you](https://nces.ed.gov/ipeds/report-your-data/resource-center-net-price) have paid in the past, after grants and scholarships are applied.
*   They can help you see whether a college is likely to offer significant **institutional aid** that brings the cost down to an affordable range.

Limitations for undocumented students:

*   Many calculators are built around federal aid formulas and may not explicitly account for undocumented status.
*   Some colleges, like [Cornell University](https://finaid.cornell.edu/financial-aid-calculators), use customized calculators that approximate institutional aid based on income and other factors, which may still be useful.

Use [NPC results](https://howtopayforcollege.com/net-price-calculators) as a **starting point**, then layer in state aid possibilities and likely private scholarships.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

*   **Assuming there is no aid at all** and giving up before researching [state and college options](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/).
*   **Applying only to expensive private or out‑of‑state schools** without strong [institutional aid policies](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2016/09/14/colleges-that-accept-undocumented-students-as-domestic-students/) for undocumented students.
*   **Missing [state Dream Act](https://www.berkeleycitycollege.edu/ucrc/resources-2/california-dream-act-ab-540-information/) or state‑aid deadlines**, which can permanently reduce your grant eligibility for a given year.
*   **Not asking colleges how they classify you** (domestic vs. international) and what that [means for aid](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/).
*   **Relying solely on scholarships** and not considering the big impact of in‑state tuition or institutional grants.
*   **Waiting until senior spring** to start scholarship searches and financial planning; many [key deadlines](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2025-26_Tip_Sheet_Undocumented_Students.pdf) are in fall or early winter.

## Common Myths

**“Undocumented students cannot go to college.”**
In most states, undocumented students can attend many colleges and universities, although some [states restrict access](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/) to certain public institutions.

**“There is no financial aid available.”**
While federal aid is off the table, many students receive [state grants, institutional aid, and private scholarships](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/), especially in inclusive states and at supportive colleges.

**“Only private scholarships are available.”**
Private scholarships are important, but they are not the only option; state financial aid and college‑funded grants can be substantial, particularly in states with [Dream Act‑style policies](https://understandingfafsa.org/undocumented-students/).

**“All colleges treat undocumented students the same.”**
Policies vary widely: some colleges treat undocumented students as [domestic applicants](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2016/09/14/colleges-that-accept-undocumented-students-as-domestic-students/) and commit to meeting full need, while others treat them as international and offer [little or no aid](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/).

***

## Additional Challenges to Be Aware Of

### Limited Access to Loans

Because federal loans are not available, undocumented students must be cautious about:

*   **Private student loans**, which often [require a credit‑worthy U.S. citizen or permanent resident cosigner](https://counselors.collegeboard.org/financial-aid/undocumented-students) and may involve high interest rates or complicated terms.
*   Relying too heavily on high‑interest debt instead of maximizing grants and scholarships.

### Documentation and Forms

You may encounter extra hurdles around documentation:

*   Providing tax or income information when parents file taxes under an [Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN)](https://www.informedimmigrant.com/resources/undocumented-students/applying-to-college-undocumented-student/) or do not file at all.
*   Filling out institutional aid forms or [state Dream Act applications](https://understandingfafsa.org/undocumented-students/) that ask about residency, high school attendance, or immigration history.

Working with counselors or community organizations familiar with these processes can make a big difference.

### Navigating Applications Without FAFSA

Even if you cannot use the FAFSA for federal aid, you may still:

*   Fill out a [paper FAFSA](https://financialaidtoolkit.ed.gov/tk/outreach/target/noncitizens.jsp) or similar form that you submit directly to a college to help them assess your financial need.
*   Complete the [CSS Profile](https://www.appily.com/guidance/articles/first-gen-underrepresented-students/undocumented-student-aid-eligibility) or a [college‑specific form](https://finaid.cornell.edu/financial-aid-calculators) used to determine institutional aid.

Always ask each college exactly which forms are required for undocumented students and where to send them.

***

## Resources and Where to Look for Help

You do not have to navigate this alone. Good places to start:

- **High school counselors or college access programs**: Many counselors now receive training on working with [undocumented and mixed‑status families](https://understandingfafsa.org/undocumented-students/) and can connect you to state and local resources.
- **College financial aid offices**: Financial aid staff can explain how their institution treats undocumented students, what aid is available, and [what forms to submit](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2025-26_Tip_Sheet_Undocumented_Students.pdf).
- **Community‑based organizations**: [Immigrant‑support groups](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/), youth organizations, and college‑access nonprofits often provide one‑on‑one advising, workshops, and scholarship lists specific to undocumented students.
- **State higher‑education agencies and immigration‑education portals**: Many states maintain updated information about tuition, [in‑state eligibility](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/), and state grants for undocumented and DACA students.

## Checklist: How to Pay for College as an Undocumented Student

Use this as a quick action checklist:

1.  **Clarify your status and goals**
    *   Know whether you are undocumented with or without DACA.
    *   Decide whether you’re aiming for a 2‑year, 4‑year, or transfer pathway.
2.  **Understand your state context**
    *   Check if your state allows [in‑state tuition](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/government/politics-elections/2025/07/18/4-things-know-about-state-tuition-noncitizens) for undocumented and DACA students.
    *   Check if your state offers [state financial aid or Dream Act‑style grants](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/).
3.  **Build a college list with affordability in mind**
    *   Include public colleges in supportive states (for in‑state tuition).
    *   Include [private colleges known for funding undocumented students](https://mydocumentedlife.org/2016/09/14/colleges-that-accept-undocumented-students-as-domestic-students/).
4.  **Talk to colleges early**
    *   Ask: “How do you classify undocumented/DACA students?”
    *   Ask: “[What forms do we submit](https://www.nasfaa.org/uploads/documents/2025-26_Tip_Sheet_Undocumented_Students.pdf) for institutional or state aid, and what are the deadlines?”
5.  **Use Net Price Calculators**
    *   Run [NPCs for each college](https://www.naicu.edu/research/federal-tool-guide/net-price-calculator/) to estimate your likely cost after institutional aid.
    *   Identify which schools look affordable before finalizing your list.
6.  **Apply for state aid (if available)**
    *   Complete your [state Dream Act](https://understandingfafsa.org/undocumented-students/) or state aid application as early as possible.
    *   Track deadlines carefully; missing them can cost you money.
7.  **Pursue scholarships aggressively**
    *   Apply to [national Dreamer‑focused scholarships](https://www.thedream.us/scholarships/).
    *   Apply to regional, local, and [school‑specific scholarships](https://getschooled.com/article/1320-scholarships-for-undocumented-daca-mented-students-get-that-money).
    *   Keep a calendar of deadlines and required essays.
8.  **Plan for work and budgeting**
    *   If you have DACA, explore [legal work opportunities](https://www.justia.com/immigration/daca/daca-and-employment/) and campus jobs where permitted.
    *   Build a basic budget for tuition, housing, food, books, and transportation.
9.  **Review offers and decide strategically**
    *   Compare [net price](https://howtopayforcollege.com/net-price-calculators) (actual out‑of‑pocket cost) across colleges, not just sticker price.
    *   Choose a school where the total package—tuition, aid, and living costs—is realistically manageable.
10. **Stay informed and seek support**
    *   Policies and programs change; keep checking for new opportunities.
    *   Stay connected to [counselors and community organizations](https://www.nilc.org/resources/toolkit-higher-education-for-immigrant-students/) that support undocumented students.
    
***

## Challenging But Within Reach

Undocumented and DACA students face real barriers: no federal grants, no federal loans, and [uneven access](https://www.higheredimmigrationportal.org/states/) to public colleges and state aid. But those barriers do **not** mean college is out of reach.

By understanding your state’s rules, targeting colleges that [support undocumented students](https://www.appily.com/guidance/articles/first-gen-underrepresented-students/undocumented-student-aid-eligibility), aggressively pursuing institutional and private scholarships, and using tools like [Net Price Calculators](https://www.naicu.edu/research/federal-tool-guide/net-price-calculator/) to compare real costs, you can build a path to an affordable degree. The process takes planning, persistence, and a willingness to ask questions—but there are many [students like you](https://immigrantsrising.org/resource/overview-of-undocumented-students/) who have done it, and many organizations and educators ready to help you do the same.
