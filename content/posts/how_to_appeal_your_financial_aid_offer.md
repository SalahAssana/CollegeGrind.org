Yes, you can ask for more financial aid.

If your financial aid offer is not enough to make a college affordable, you may be able to [ask the school to review it](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college). Colleges often have formal [appeal or “special circumstances” processes](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/), and financial aid offices expect that some students will need to appeal each year. An appeal is never guaranteed, but when you have a clear reason and good documentation, it is often worth trying because [schools can sometimes adjust aid](https://studentaid.gov/articles/financial-aid-not-enough/) based on [updated information](https://www.mefa.org/article/need-financial-aid-learn-appeal-process/).

This guide walks through when it makes sense to appeal, how the process usually works, what information schools consider, and how to write a clear, respectful appeal.

## When You Should and Shouldn’t Appeal

### When It Makes Sense to Appeal

Appeals are most effective when something important has changed or was not fully captured in your original financial aid applications.

Common situations where an appeal can make sense include:

* Your family’s income has dropped because of job loss, reduced hours, or business downturn.
* Your household has taken on significant medical expenses not covered by insurance.
* Your family situation has changed (divorce, separation, death of a parent, new dependents, or multiple children now in college).
* Your FAFSA or CSS Profile did not reflect [special circumstances](https://www.mefa.org/article/need-financial-aid-learn-appeal-process/) such as one-time income, recent relocation costs, or other [unusual expenses](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college).
* You received a [noticeably stronger offer](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package) from a comparable college, and your preferred school allows you to [submit competing offers](https://www.thecollegefundingcoach.org/how-to-write-a-letter-of-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-and-merit-funds/) for review.

In these cases, you are giving the financial aid office new or clearer information, which is exactly what they use [“professional judgment”](https://studentaid.gov/help-center/answers/article/how-can-i-get-more-aid) to review.

### When an Appeal Is Less Likely to Work

There are also situations where an appeal is less likely to lead to changes:

- You have no new information, and your financial situation is essentially the same as what you reported.
- The school already meets full need according to its own formula and is known to be very strict about changes.
- You are [asking only for more merit aid](https://blog.getintocollege.com/asking-prospective-colleges-for-more-merit-aid/) without new academic achievements, awards, or a [comparable better offer](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package) to point to.

Appeals work best when you can [clearly show why the original numbers do not reflect your real, current financial situation](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college). Wanting more aid by itself is not enough.

## Understand What Can Be Adjusted

Financial aid offices generally cannot change federal or state rules, but they can [use discretion (often called professional judgment)](https://studentaid.gov/help-center/answers/article/how-can-i-get-more-aid) to [update the data they use](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college) when calculating your eligibility.

Depending on the school and your situation, they may reconsider:

* Need-based aid, by updating income or household information in your FAFSA/CSS Profile data.
* [Special financial circumstances](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/), such as high medical bills, recent unemployment, or [one-time income events](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college).
* In some cases, institutional grants or scholarships, especially when a [competing offer is involved](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package).

However, schools are [not required to change your offer](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/), even if you appeal with documentation. [Funding limits and institutional policies](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package) can all affect how flexible a college can be.

## Step 1: Review Your Financial Aid Offer Carefully

Before you appeal, make sure you clearly understand your existing offer.

Start by breaking down your financial aid award letter so you can see:

* Grants and scholarships (money you do not have to repay).
* Federal and institutional loans (money you must repay, usually after graduation).
* Federal work-study or campus employment.

Then identify your **net price**: the college’s [total cost of attendance](https://studentaid.gov/help-center/answers/article/how-can-i-get-more-aid) (tuition, fees, housing, meals, books, transportation, and personal expenses) minus grants and scholarships. This shows [how much you and your family are expected to cover](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/financial-aid-appeal-letter) with savings, income, and loans.

If you are unsure how to read your award letter, review a detailed guide on understanding financial aid award letters before you start your appeal so you know exactly what you are asking the school to reconsider.

## Step 2: Identify Your Reason for Appealing

Once you know what your current package looks like, clarify **why** you are appealing. This will shape what you say and what you need to document.

### Common Valid Reasons

- **Job loss or reduced income:** A parent or student has been laid off, had hours cut, or lost a major source of income.
- **Unexpected expenses:** [Large medical bills](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college), natural disaster costs, or other [necessary expenses](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/) that strain your budget.
- **Change in family situation:** [Divorce, separation, death of an income earner](https://www.mefa.org/article/need-financial-aid-learn-appeal-process/), a new dependent, or [multiple children now enrolled in college](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college).
- **Better financial aid offer from another school:** Another college with a similar profile has [offered significantly more grant or scholarship aid](https://www.thecollegefundingcoach.org/how-to-write-a-letter-of-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-and-merit-funds/), and your preferred school is open to [reviewing competing offers](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package).
- **Errors or missing information on your FAFSA/CSS Profile:** [Mistakes or omissions](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/financial-aid-appeal-letter) that made your [situation look stronger](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college) than it really is.

### Key Tip: Be Specific and Honest

When you appeal, clearly explain **what changed**, **when it changed**, and **how it affects your ability to pay**. Financial aid offices generally respond best to [concise, factual explanations](https://www.thrivent.com/insights/college-planning/how-to-write-a-financial-aid-appeal-letter) instead of [broad statements](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/financial-aid-appeal-letter) like “we cannot afford this.”

## Step 3: Gather Documentation

Strong documentation is one of the most important parts of a successful appeal.

Depending on your situation, you might collect:

* Recent pay stubs, termination letters, or proof of reduced hours.
* Updated tax returns or amended returns if your income is now lower.
* [Medical bills, insurance statements](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/), or payment plans showing [out-of-pocket costs](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college).
* Court documents or legal agreements related to divorce, separation, or child support.
* Letters from employers confirming job loss or major changes in compensation.
* [Official financial aid award letters](https://www.thecollegefundingcoach.org/how-to-write-a-letter-of-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-and-merit-funds/) from other colleges if you plan to [reference competing offers](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college).

Attach only what is necessary to support your explanation, and keep everything organized. [Clear, complete documentation](https://www.thrivent.com/insights/college-planning/how-to-write-a-financial-aid-appeal-letter) makes it easier for the financial aid office to review your case and [can speed up the process](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/financial-aid-appeal-letter).

## Step 4: Contact the Financial Aid Office

Before writing your full appeal, check how your school wants you to start.

Many colleges [outline their appeal process](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college) on the financial aid section of their website and may call it a [special circumstances review](https://www.mefa.org/article/need-financial-aid-learn-appeal-process/) or professional judgment review. Some schools use an online form, while others ask for an emailed or mailed letter.

Steps to begin:

* Look up the college’s financial aid appeal instructions and note any deadlines.
* If anything is unclear, call or email the financial aid office and ask how to submit an appeal and what they expect from you.
* Ask if there is a [preferred person or title](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/financial-aid-appeal-letter) to address your letter to (for example, the [Director of Financial Aid](https://www.mefa.org/article/need-financial-aid-learn-appeal-process/)).

When you reach out, keep your tone polite and professional. You are asking for help, not demanding it.

## Step 5: Write Your Financial Aid Appeal Letter

Your appeal letter does not need to be long or formal, but it should be [clear, respectful, and well organized](https://www.uc.edu/blog/how-to-write-financial-aid-appeal-letter.html). Many colleges and financial experts recommend keeping these letters [short, honest, and focused on the facts](https://www.thrivent.com/insights/college-planning/how-to-write-a-financial-aid-appeal-letter).

### What to Include

Try using this simple structure:

1.  **Introduction and thanks**
    * State your name, student ID, and the program you were admitted to.
    * Thank the school for your admission and the aid you have already been offered.

2.  **Clear reason for appealing**
    * Briefly explain the [main reason for your appeal](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/financial-aid-appeal-letter) (job loss, new medical expenses, family change, competing offer, etc.).
    * Mention when the change happened.

3.  **Specific details and impact**
    * Provide 1–3 short paragraphs describing how this situation affects your family’s ability to pay.
    * [Refer to documentation you are attaching](https://www.thrivent.com/insights/college-planning/how-to-write-a-financial-aid-appeal-letter) (for example, “I have attached a copy of my parent’s termination letter dated March 15”).

4.  **What you are asking for**
    * Politely ask the financial aid office to [reconsider your aid](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/financial-aid-appeal-letter) based on the updated information.
    * You do not need to request a specific dollar amount unless the school encourages it; you can simply ask for “any additional grant aid or scholarship consideration you may be able to offer.”

5.  **Optional: Competing offer**
    * If appropriate and permitted by the school, briefly [mention a more generous financial aid offer](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package) from a comparable college and [attach a copy of that offer](https://www.thecollegefundingcoach.org/how-to-write-a-letter-of-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-and-merit-funds/).
    * Emphasize that the school you are appealing to is your top choice but that cost is the barrier to attending.

6.  **Closing**
    * Reaffirm your appreciation for their time and consideration.
    * Provide your contact information and invite them to reach out if they need additional details.

### What to Avoid

To keep your appeal as strong as possible, avoid:

* **Demands or ultimatums**, such as “If you do not increase my aid by this amount, I will not attend.”
* **Emotional or aggressive language**, including blaming or [criticizing the college](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package).
* **Vague or unsupported claims**, like “we have a lot of bills” without explaining or documenting them.
* **Exaggerations or inaccurate information**; [dishonest appeals](https://www.thrivent.com/insights/college-planning/how-to-write-a-financial-aid-appeal-letter) can backfire and may have serious consequences.

Focus on being honest, brief, and specific. Let your documentation do much of the work.

## Step 6: Follow Up and Be Patient

After you submit your appeal and documentation, schools may need time to review everything. The timeline varies by college, but [decisions can take anywhere from a few days to several weeks](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package), especially during peak seasons around deposit deadlines and the start of a term.

Many schools will contact you if they need [additional information or clarification](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/financial-aid-appeal-letter). Respond promptly and [supply any requested documents](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college) as soon as you can.

If you have not heard back within the timeframe the school gave you—or within about two weeks if no timeframe was specified—it is reasonable to follow up with a brief, polite email or call asking for an update.

## What Happens After You Appeal

Once your appeal is reviewed, several outcomes are possible:

- **Increased grant or scholarship aid:** The school may offer [additional institutional grants or scholarships](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/), or [adjust your need-based award](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college) based on updated information.
- **Adjusted loan or work options:** The college might increase work-study eligibility or adjust the mix of loans available to you.
- **No change to your aid:** Sometimes, even with a strong case, the [school may not be able to offer additional funding](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/) due to [budget limits or policy constraints](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package).

Some research suggests that a [significant share of appeals at many colleges do result in more aid](https://blog.getintocollege.com/asking-prospective-colleges-for-more-merit-aid/), often amounting to several thousand dollars per year, but the [exact outcome can vary widely](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/) by institution and by student. It is important to go into the process hopeful but realistic.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Appealing is not complicated, but there are common missteps that can weaken your case.

Avoid:

- **Waiting too long to appeal:** Some schools have [priority deadlines](https://www.fastweb.com/financial-aid/articles/three-tips-to-appeal-financial-aid-packages), and funds can run out. [Appealing as soon as your circumstances change](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college) or you receive your award letter can improve your chances.
- **Not providing documentation:** Telling your story without proof makes it hard for the financial aid office to justify adjustments.
- **Being vague:** General statements like “college is expensive” are less persuasive than clear numbers and specific situations.
- **Comparing offers incorrectly:** Expecting a highly selective, high-demand school to match a much less selective college’s aid is often unrealistic.
- **Assuming approval is guaranteed:** An appeal is a request, not a right, and even [strong cases are sometimes denied](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/paying-for-college/articles/strategies-for-appealing-a-college-financial-aid-package) due to [budget limits](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/).

## It’s Always Worth Asking If You Have a Case

Appealing your financial aid offer is a normal, [expected part of the process](https://www.mefa.org/article/need-financial-aid-learn-appeal-process/) at many colleges, especially when families [experience changes](https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/how-to-appeal-for-more-financial-aid-for-college) between filing the FAFSA and receiving offers.

If paying for college is a real concern and you have clear reasons backed by documentation, there is usually little downside to politely asking your school to reconsider your aid. Even if the answer is no, you will know you advocated for yourself and explored every option.

If you still cannot make the numbers work after appealing, remember that there are [other strategies](https://studentaid.gov/articles/financial-aid-not-enough/): applying for outside scholarships, exploring lower-cost schools, considering community college, or [using payment plans and work options](https://www.affordablecollegesonline.org/college-resource-center/appeal-financial-aid/) suggested by your financial aid and college planning resources.
