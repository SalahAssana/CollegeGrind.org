When you start planning your high school schedule, it does not take long to run into terms like "Honors" and "AP." You probably already know that both are considered [harder classes than standard high school courses](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/). But if you are trying to map out your classes, you might wonder: if both are advanced options, what actually makes them different?

Here's where things get confusing. Many students assume that AP is simply a higher level of Honors, or that taking an AP class [automatically guarantees you college credit](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/). Neither of those assumptions is true.

The biggest difference to remember comes down to who creates the class and how it is standardized. Honors classes are faster-paced, more challenging versions of regular high school courses designed and managed by your local school or district. Advanced Placement (AP) classes are specific, college-level courses created by a national organization called the [College Board](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/about-ap/ap-a-glance).

Understanding how these two tracks work can help you build a strong schedule without taking on unnecessary stress. Whether you are planning for freshman year or trying to decide on senior electives, this guide breaks down everything you need to know about Advanced High School Classes Explained.

An Honors class is usually a more rigorous, faster-paced version of a standard high school course. High schools offer Honors options in core subjects like English, algebra, biology, chemistry, and world history.

The main defining feature of an Honors class is that it is created and managed locally. There is no national organization that sets a universal Honors curriculum. Instead, your local school district, high school academic department, or even an individual teacher decides what goes into the syllabus.

Because Honors courses are defined locally, their [difficulty and content vary significantly](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/) from one school to another. An Honors Chemistry class at a large suburban high school might focus heavily on advanced lab reports and mathematical calculations, while an Honors Chemistry class in another district might focus more on independent research projects.

Despite these differences, Honors classes generally share a few common traits across most high schools:

* They cover the same fundamental subject matter as regular classes, but in greater depth.
* They move at a faster pace, requiring more independent reading and homework.
* They emphasize critical thinking, complex problem-solving, and analytical writing.
* They may require prerequisites, such as strong grades in prior classes or a teacher recommendation.

When educators talk about [course rigor](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/)—which simply means how challenging your academic schedule is—Honors classes represent an important step up from standard high school coursework. To learn more about how local course options are structured, check out our guide on Honor Classes Explained.

Advanced Placement (AP) is a national program created and administered by the [College Board](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/about-ap/ap-a-glance)—the same non-profit organization that gives the SAT. Unlike Honors classes, AP classes are designed to provide actual college-level academic work while you are still in high school.

Every AP course follows an official set of expectations called an [AP Course and Exam Description framework](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/about-ap/ap-a-glance). These frameworks are created by committees of college professors and experienced high school teachers to match the scope and academic demands of introductory college courses. Currently, the College Board offers 40 official AP courses across seven major subject areas, including math, science, history, English, world languages, fine arts, and computer science.

To officially offer an AP course, a high school teacher must submit their course syllabus to the College Board for an annual review called the [AP Course Audit](https://international.collegeboard.org/toolkit/ap-policy/understanding-ap). Once approved, the class gains authorization to use the official "AP" label on student transcripts.

Each AP course concludes in May with a national [standardized exam](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/about-ap/ap-a-glance). These exams are given on the same dates worldwide and feature multiple-choice questions along with free-response sections like analytical essays or complex problem sets. AP exams are graded on a 1-to-5 scale by college faculty and expert educators:

* **5**: Extremely well qualified (equivalent to an A in a college course)
* **4**: Well qualified (equivalent to grades of A-, B+, or B)
* **3**: Qualified (considered a passing score by the College Board)
* **2**: Possibly qualified
* **1**: No recommendation

It is crucial to understand the distinction between taking an AP *course* and taking the AP *exam*. Your grade in the AP class appears on your high school report card and affects your high school GPA. Your score on the May AP exam is separate.

Don't assume that enrolling in an AP class automatically guarantees you [college credit](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/). Earning **college credit**—which means receiving credit hours toward a college degree—depends on your May exam score and the specific policies of the college you end up attending. For a deeper look into subject options and test structures, explore our full breakdown on AP Classes Explained.

The core difference between Honors and AP comes down to [national standardization and postsecondary connection](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/).

Honors describes an advanced high school course created by your local school. AP is a standardized national program designed to mirror introductory college work and evaluate learning through a single end-of-year national test.

| Feature | Honors Classes | AP Classes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What is it?** | More rigorous high school coursework. | College Board advanced coursework program. |
| **Nationally standardized?** | No; curriculum is set locally. | Yes; AP courses follow [College Board frameworks](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/). |
| **Standardized exam?** | Generally no national standardized exam. | Yes; nationwide [AP exam scored 1–5 in May](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/about-ap/ap-a-glance). |
| **College-level coursework?** | Generally more advanced high school work. | Designed to be comparable to introductory college-level work. |
| **Potential college credit?** | Generally no mechanism for college credit. | Yes, depending on the college and exam score. |
| **Course difficulty** | Varies significantly by school and teacher. | Rigorous, but student experience still varies. |
| **GPA weighting** | Depends on school policy (often +0.5). | Depends on school policy (often +1.0). |
| **College admissions value** | Can demonstrate course rigor in local context. | Can demonstrate course rigor and [college readiness](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/about-ap/ap-a-glance). |

This structural difference affects everything from how assignments are graded to how colleges look at your transcript. Because Honors classes are flexible, teachers can adjust their pacing if the class needs extra help on a difficult concept. AP teachers must stick to a strict schedule to cover all required material before the national exam in May.

## **Is AP Harder Than Honors?**

The short answer is: usually, but not always.

Saying "AP is always harder than Honors" is [too simplistic](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/). AP classes are intended to be more challenging because they cover introductory college-level material at a fast pace. However, real-world course difficulty depends on several factors:

* **The Subject**: An AP subject that relies heavily on memorization, like AP Psychology or AP Human Geography, might feel easier to you than an intensive, math-heavy Honors Physics or Honors Precalculus course.
* **The Teacher and School**: Because Honors classes are created locally, an ambitious Honors teacher at a competitive high school might assign more reading and writing than an AP teacher at another school.
* **Pacing Requirements**: AP courses have a hard deadline—the [May exam date](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/about-ap/ap-a-glance). AP teachers must move quickly through dense material, which creates a sustained, heavy workload throughout the year. Honors teachers have more flexibility to slow down when needed.
* **Your Personal Strengths**: If you excel at writing and reading primary sources, AP U.S. History might feel manageable, while Honors Chemistry might require twice as much study time.

Instead of assuming AP is automatically harder, think of Honors as an accelerated high school class and AP as a fast-paced college course. Both will push you, but AP adds the pressure of covering a rigid national syllabus before May.

## **Honors vs. AP for College Admissions**

When applying to college, families often ask: do admissions officers prefer Honors or AP classes?

Admissions offices value both pathways because both demonstrate that you chose to challenge yourself rather than taking the easiest path available. However, colleges do not evaluate your choices in isolation. They evaluate your course choices in the context of [what your high school actually offers](https://www.shemmassianconsulting.com/blog/weighted-vs-unweighted-gpa).

Every high school sends an official document called a High School Profile to colleges along with your application. This profile tells admissions officers which advanced classes, honors options, and AP courses are available at your school.

Here is what this means for you:

* **If your school offers many AP classes**: Selective colleges expect to see that you took advantage of those opportunities by taking AP or Honors courses in your core academic subjects.
* **If your school offers few or no AP classes**: Admissions officers will not penalize you. They will look to see if you took the highest level of coursework available to you, such as Honors or local dual enrollment.

Colleges also weigh the balance between course challenge and your actual grades. A common dilemma for students is whether an A in an Honors class is better than a B in an AP class. Admissions officers at [competitive universities](https://orieladmissions.com/how-many-ap-classes-for-yale/) often say they want to see both challenge and strong grades. Earning a B in a tough AP class shows you are willing to stretch yourself academically. However, earning C or D grades in multiple AP classes can hurt your overall transcript.

## **Do Honors or AP Classes Give College Credit?**

The ability to earn postsecondary credit is one of the clearest practical differences between [Honors and AP coursework](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/).

Honors classes generally do not provide college credit. Fulfilling Honors requirements earns you high school credit toward graduation and shows academic strength on your transcript, but colleges do not convert high school Honors completion into college degree hours.

AP classes, on the other hand, offer a standardized pathway to earn college credit or **placement**. Placement means a college allows you to skip an introductory requirement and enroll directly in an upper-level course as a freshman, even if they do not grant actual degree credit hours.

Whether you get college credit for an AP exam depends entirely on the policy of the college you attend. There is no universal national rule.

Consider how credit policies vary across different types of institutions:

* **Large Public Universities**: Public state universities, such as the University of Texas at Austin or UCLA, are generally [generous with AP credit](https://calcilo.com/us/blog/ap-score-for-college-credit/). At UT Austin, for example, earning a score of 3, 4, or 5 on many AP exams satisfies lower-division course requirements, awarding actual college credit hours on your university transcript according to the [UT Testing and Evaluation Services](https://testingservices.utexas.edu/search-undergraduate-exams).
* **Selective Private Universities**: Selective institutions like Princeton University or Yale University have [more restrictive policies](https://orieladmissions.com/how-many-ap-classes-for-yale/). They often require a top exam score of 4 or 5 to grant credit, or they may use AP scores primarily for course placement rather than granting credit hours that let you graduate early.
* **Specific Academic Majors**: Credit policies can also vary by department within the same university. An engineering department might require a score of 5 on AP Calculus BC to grant math credit, while the humanities department at the same school might accept a score of 3 or 4 for general elective credit.

Always check the specific AP credit policy on a college's official website rather than assuming every school treats your exam score the same way.

## **How Honors and AP Affect GPA**

Your grade point average (GPA) is one of the key numbers on your high school transcript. High schools calculate GPA using two main methods: unweighted and weighted scales.

An **unweighted GPA** measures your grades on a standard 4.0 scale, regardless of how hard the class is:

* A = 4.0
* B = 3.0
* C = 2.0
* D = 1.0
* F = 0.0

A **weighted GPA** gives extra points for taking more challenging classes, rewarding students who tackle [difficult coursework](https://talk.collegeconfidential.com/t/do-i-have-a-chance-at-uf/3699579).

While weighted formulas vary by school district, many high schools use a system similar to this:

* **Standard Classes**: Graded on a [standard 4.0 scale](https://mekreview.com/ap-exams-101-everything-you-need-to-know/).
* **Honors Classes**: Add an extra 0.5 point boost (A = 4.5, B = 3.5, C = 2.5).
* **AP Classes**: Add an extra 1.0 point boost (A = 5.0, B = 4.0, C = 3.0).

Under this system, getting a B in an AP class (4.0 weighted) yields the same weighted GPA points as getting an A in a standard class (4.0 unweighted).

However, don't pick classes simply to chase GPA boosts. Because every high school district sets its own GPA weighting rules, many college admissions offices recalculate GPAs on their own scale. For example, the University of Michigan recalculates all applicant GPAs to an unweighted 4.0 scale using core academic grades, while evaluating course rigor separately during transcript review. Other state institutions, like the [University of Florida](https://talk.collegeconfidential.com/t/do-i-have-a-chance-at-uf/3699579), recalculate GPAs using a standardized state weighting model.

Check your high school student handbook to learn how your specific school calculates weighted grades.

## **Can You Take Honors Before AP?**

Yes, taking an Honors class before an AP class is a very common course sequence. In fact, many high schools design their curriculum tracks so that Honors classes serve as direct preparation for later AP courses.

Common prerequisite sequences include:

* **Math**: Honors Algebra II -> Honors Precalculus -> AP Calculus AB or BC.
* **Science**: Honors Chemistry -> AP Chemistry, or Honors Biology -> AP Biology.
* **World Languages**: Honors Spanish III or IV -> AP Spanish Language and Culture.

Taking Honors first helps you build foundational subject knowledge and study skills before stepping up to college-level AP expectations.

However, taking Honors first is not a universal rule. In subjects like history, social sciences, or introductory electives, high schools often allow qualified students to jump directly into AP classes. For example, students often take AP Human Geography, AP Psychology, or AP European History as their first AP class without a specific Honors prerequisite in that exact subject.

Prerequisite rules are set locally by high schools, so ask your school counselor about local placement policies.

## **Can You Take Honors and AP at the Same Time?**

You do not have to pick just one track for all your classes. High school schedules are flexible, and most advanced students take a [combination of standard, Honors, and AP courses](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/) during the same school year.

Mixing course levels allows you to challenge yourself in subjects you enjoy while keeping your overall workload manageable.

Here is an example of a balanced junior-year schedule:

| Class Period | Subject | Course Level | Why This Choice Works |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Period 1** | English | AP English Language | Strong reading and writing skills; ready for [college-level writing](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/). |
| **Period 2** | History | AP U.S. History | High interest in history; ready for primary source analysis. |
| **Period 3** | Math | Honors Precalculus | Solid math foundation; preparing for senior-year AP Calculus. |
| **Period 4** | Science | Honors Chemistry | Wants a strong lab science without the heavy AP Chemistry load. |
| **Period 5** | Language | Spanish III | Standard level to consolidate language proficiency. |
| **Period 6** | Elective | Art History or Band | Standard level to balance academic stress and maintain personal interests. |

Taking a balanced schedule prevents burnout and ensures you have time for extracurricular activities, sports, jobs, and sleep.

## **Which Should You Take?**

Deciding whether to sign up for Honors or AP comes down to your academic preparation, subject interest, and personal goals. Neither option is automatically better for every student.

### **Honors May Be the Right Choice When:**

* You want a more challenging class than standard level, but are not ready for a fast-paced college workload.
* You are building foundational skills in a subject before taking an AP class in a future grade.
* An AP course in that subject is too demanding for your current schedule.
* Honors is the highest level offered by your school in that particular subject.
* You want academic challenge without the pressure of a high-stakes national exam in May.

### **AP May Be the Right Choice When:**

* You are academically prepared and have performed well in previous Honors or standard classes in that subject.
* You have a genuine interest in the subject and want to explore college-level material.
* You want to demonstrate strong course rigor to [competitive colleges](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/about-ap/ap-a-glance).
* You have good time management skills and can handle a heavy homework load.
* You want the opportunity to earn potential college credit or placement.

Use this practical decision framework to guide your choices, and talk with your teachers and school counselor before finalizing your schedule. For step-by-step advice on balancing your schedule, see How to Choose Your High School Classes.

## **What If Your School Doesn't Offer AP?**

High schools vary widely in size and resources. While some large high schools offer 20 or more AP courses, many small, rural, or specialized schools offer only a few, or none at all.

If your school does not offer AP classes, [do not panic](https://orieladmissions.com/how-many-ap-classes-for-yale/).

Colleges judge your application based on the context of your high school. Admissions officers do not expect you to take courses that your school does not offer. If your high school's highest academic level is its Honors program, taking those Honors classes shows colleges that you are challenging yourself as much as possible.

If you want additional academic challenge beyond what your school catalog offers, consider these alternative options:

* **Dual Enrollment**: Taking classes at a local community college or university while in high school.
* **Other Advanced Programs**: Participating in programs like [International Baccalaureate (IB)](https://www.sparkadmissions.com/blog/ib-vs-ap/) or Cambridge AICE if available in your district.
* **Online Advanced Classes**: Enrolling in accredited online state options approved by your guidance counselor.

You do not need to manufacture AP classes if your school does not offer them. Focus on doing well in the most challenging courses available to you.

## **What About Dual Enrollment, IB, and Other Advanced Classes?**

AP and Honors are the two most common advanced options in American high schools, but they are not the only ones. Depending on your school district, you might encounter other advanced academic programs:

* **Dual Enrollment**: Programs that allow high school students to take real college courses at a local community college or university. Unlike AP, where credit depends on an exam score, dual enrollment earns you actual college credit directly through a college transcript grade. Read more in our guide on Dual Enrollment.
* **International Baccalaureate (IB)**: A global, two-year diploma program for juniors and seniors. While AP allows you to pick standalone courses, the full [IB program](https://www.sparkadmissions.com/blog/ib-vs-ap/) requires a comprehensive, interdisciplinary schedule including independent research and community service. Learn more in our breakdown of the IB Diploma Programme.
* **Cambridge AICE**: An international pre-university curriculum developed by Cambridge University. It offers subject-based examinations similar to A-Levels, allowing students to earn advanced standing or college credit at participating universities according to [Broward College's Cambridge equivalencies](https://www.broward.edu/admissions/credit-for-prior-learning/cambridge-international-exams.html). See our guide on Cambridge AICE.
* **Career & Technical Education (CTE)**: Specialized tracks that combine academic instruction with practical, hands-on training for specific career fields and technical certifications. Explore options in Career & Technical Education.

Understanding that "advanced classes" is a broad category helps you put Honors and AP into perspective.

## **Common Myths About Honors and AP**

### **Myth 1: "AP is just Honors with a harder name."**

**Fact**: AP is a distinct national program created by the [College Board](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/), featuring standardized syllabi and national May exams. Honors classes are created and managed locally by individual high schools.

### **Myth 2: "Every AP class is harder than every Honors class."**

**Fact**: Course difficulty varies based on the subject, teacher standards, pacing, and your individual academic background. An intensive Honors science or math class can demand more time than an introductory AP class.

### **Myth 3: "You need AP classes to get into a good college."**

**Fact**: Colleges evaluate your course selection within the context of your high school's available offerings. If your school offers Honors classes but no APs, excelling in [Honors coursework](https://orieladmissions.com/how-many-ap-classes-for-yale/) demonstrates strong course rigor.

### **Myth 4: "Taking an AP class guarantees college credit."**

**Fact**: Enrolling in an AP class does not guarantee college credit. Credit awards depend on earning a qualifying score (usually 3, 4, or 5) on the May AP exam and depend on the [specific credit policies](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/) of the college you attend.

### **Myth 5: "More AP classes always make your application better."**

**Fact**: Admissions officers value quality of performance over pure volume. Taking too many AP classes and earning C or D grades hurts your transcript far more than taking a balanced schedule and earning solid A grades. Read our strategic advice on How Many AP Classes Should You Take?.

### **Myth 6: "Honors classes don't matter for college admissions."**

**Fact**: Honors classes show colleges that you are taking on challenging coursework, building strong foundations, and preparing for higher-level study.

### **Myth 7: "Honors always adds 0.5 GPA points and AP always adds 1.0."**

**Fact**: GPA weighting formulas are set locally by school districts. Some high schools do not weight GPAs at all, while others use [different point systems](https://www.shemmassianconsulting.com/blog/weighted-vs-unweighted-gpa). Colleges also recalculate GPAs using their own formulas.

## **Frequently Asked Questions**

### **Is AP harder than Honors?**

AP classes are generally designed to be more rigorous because they cover introductory college material on a strict national timeline. However, actual difficulty varies by subject, teacher standards, and your individual strengths.

### **Is Honors considered an advanced class?**

Yes. Honors classes are accelerated secondary courses that cover material in greater depth and at a faster pace than standard high school classes.

### **Do colleges care about Honors classes?**

Yes. Admissions offices review your transcript to see if you challenged yourself with available rigorous coursework. Performing well in Honors courses demonstrates academic engagement and subject preparation.

### **Do Honors classes give college credit?**

Generally, no. Honors classes fulfill high school graduation requirements and demonstrate course rigor, but they do not have a standardized national credit-transfer mechanism.

### **Do AP classes give college credit?**

AP classes can yield [college credit or course placement](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/) if you earn a qualifying score on the national May AP exam and meet the credit criteria of your specific college.

### **Can I take AP without taking Honors first?**

In many subjects—especially history, social sciences, and electives—students can enroll in AP classes directly without an Honors prerequisite. Sequential subjects like math and science usually require completing lower-level Honors prerequisites first.

### **Should I take Honors or AP?**

Select Honors if you want to build foundational skills, manage a busy extracurricular schedule, or take challenging work without a high-stakes national May exam. Select AP if you are academically prepared, interested in the subject, and want to tackle college-level work for potential college credit.

### **Can I take Honors and AP classes at the same time?**

Yes. Taking a mixed schedule of Honors, AP, and standard courses is standard practice and helps maintain academic balance.

### **Does AP raise your GPA more than Honors?**

In school districts that use [weighted GPAs](https://talk.collegeconfidential.com/t/do-i-have-a-chance-at-uf/3699579), AP classes often award a higher point boost (such as +1.0) than Honors classes (such as +0.5). Check your school handbook to confirm local GPA policies.

### **What if my school doesn't offer AP?**

Colleges evaluate your application within the [context of what your high school offers](https://www.shemmassianconsulting.com/blog/weighted-vs-unweighted-gpa). You will not be penalized if AP courses are unavailable at your school.

### **Is an A in Honors better than a B in AP?**

Admissions officers value both [challenge and strong grades](https://orieladmissions.com/how-many-ap-classes-for-yale/). A grade of B in an AP class shows you tackled a college-level course, which is respected. Earning an A in Honors is better than earning low grades (C or D) in an AP class.

### **How many AP or Honors classes should I take?**

There is no universal number required for college admission. Focus on creating a sustainable schedule where you can earn strong grades while maintaining your personal well-being and extracurricular interests.

## **Similar But Not The Same**

Honors and AP are both ways to take more challenging classes in high school, but they are not the same thing. Honors usually means a more rigorous version of a high school course, while AP is a specific [College Board program](https://www.mcmillaneducation.com/blog/ap-classes-explained/) that can also lead to college credit or placement depending on the exam score and college.
