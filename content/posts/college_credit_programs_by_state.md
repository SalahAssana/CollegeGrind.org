Secondary education in the United States has undergone a quiet structural transformation over the past two decades. High school students no longer need to wait until graduation day to step onto a college campus or begin earning postsecondary credits. Through [state-legislated programs](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/), regional institutional partnerships, and local school district initiatives, millions of high school students now complete college coursework alongside their traditional high school diplomas each year.

However, earning postsecondary credit while in high school is not governed by a single uniform national system. Instead, early college opportunities represent a patchwork landscape dictated by state statutes, higher education funding frameworks, school district policies, and individual university transfer rules. A course that is completely tuition-free and automatically transferable to a state university for a student in [Washington](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf) or Virginia may carry out-of-pocket tuition costs or restricted transferability for a student living in another state.

Understanding how these programs operate, identifying state-specific opportunities, and evaluating how earned credits apply toward a future degree are essential steps for students and families navigating college planning.

## **What Are College Credit Programs in High School?**

College credit programs in high school allow secondary students to enroll in postsecondary coursework and earn credit that counts simultaneously toward high school graduation and a college degree. This dual-award process is known as [dual transcription](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf) or dual credit.

When a student successfully completes an approved college course while enrolled in high school, [two distinct academic records](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf) are generated:

* **High School Transcript**: The completed course is recorded on the student's high school transcript to fulfill [local diploma requirements](https://www.fldoe.org/schools/higher-ed/fl-college-system/dual-enroll-transfer/) (such as substituting a college general psychology course for a high school social studies elective).
* **Permanent Postsecondary Transcript**: The participating college or university issues an official [postsecondary transcript](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf) containing the course title, credit hours, and letter grade earned.

**Important Lesson:** Because these courses generate an official college transcript, performance in dual enrollment classes establishes a [permanent postsecondary grade point average (GPA)](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf). Grades earned follow the student throughout their academic career, directly influencing future undergraduate admission options, academic standing, and eligibility for federal or state financial aid.

Postsecondary credit programs in high school operate across three primary [instructional settings](https://www.cwu.edu/academics/specialized-programs/college-high-school/_documents/ospi-faqs.pdf):

1. **College Campuses**: Students attend traditional college classes alongside matriculated undergraduate students, taught directly by university or community college faculty.
2. **High School Campuses**: Courses are delivered inside the high school during the regular school day, taught either by qualified high school teachers approved as adjunct college faculty or by visiting college instructors.
3. **Distance and Online Learning**: Courses are completed through synchronous or asynchronous [online platforms](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf) administered directly by the partnering postsecondary institution.

## **What Do States Mean by Dual Enrollment, Dual Credit, and Concurrent Enrollment?**

One of the primary sources of confusion for students and families is [terminology](https://www.nacep.org/resource-center/ecs-50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/). States, school districts, and higher education institutions frequently use different terms to describe similar administrative arrangements—or use the exact same term to describe entirely different funding and delivery models.

| Term | Operational Definition | Common Geographic Context | Primary Delivery Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dual Enrollment** | Broad administrative umbrella covering any instance where a secondary student takes [postsecondary coursework](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/). | Used nationwide across state statutes. | Encompasses campus-based, online, and high-school-based college courses. |
| **Dual Credit** | Refers specifically to the dual-award mechanism: earning high school diploma credit and postsecondary credit for [one class](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf). | Texas, Illinois, Missouri, [North Carolina](https://nccareers.org/career-college-promise?lang=ar). | Focuses on credit transcript alignment rather than physical class location. |
| **Concurrent Enrollment** | College courses taught on a high school campus by high school teachers credentialed as [college adjuncts](https://www.cwu.edu/academics/specialized-programs/college-high-school/_documents/ospi-faqs.pdf). | Minnesota, Colorado, Washington, NACEP accredited programs. | Students maintain a high school schedule while completing college-level coursework. |
| **College in the High School (CiHS)** | Formal statutory term for high-school-based [concurrent enrollment](https://www.cwu.edu/academics/specialized-programs/college-high-school/_documents/ospi-faqs.pdf). | Washington, Minnesota, Pennsylvania. | Taught during the high school day; tuition is frequently subsidized or free under state law. |
| **Postsecondary Enrollment Options (PSEO)** | State-mandated programs allowing high school students to take classes on college campuses [full- or part-time](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf). | Minnesota (flagship model), Ohio, Michigan. | State funds follow the student to cover postsecondary tuition, textbooks, and fees. |
| **Running Start** | Specific state-legislated campus enrollment model for [11th and 12th graders](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf). | Washington, Hawaii, Alaska. | Students take tuition-free courses at local community and technical colleges. |
| **Early College High School** | Comprehensive 4-to-5 year high school model blending secondary and [postsecondary curricula](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/). | North Carolina, Texas, California, Massachusetts, Michigan. | Designed for students to earn a high school diploma and up to 60 college credits or an associate degree [simultaneously](https://nccareers.org/career-college-promise?lang=ar). |
| **CTE Dual Credit** | Career and Technical Education pathways granting technical college credit for [vocational coursework](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf). | Statewide programs in Virginia, North Carolina, Washington. | Aligns high school vocational tracks with industry certificates and technical associate degrees. |

Two programs sharing the exact same name in different states may operate under completely different financial and legal frameworks. For example, "Concurrent Enrollment" in Colorado is a statewide entitlement program established by statute, whereas in other states the term simply describes an informal local agreement between a high school and a community college.

## **How College Credit Programs Differ From AP, IB, and AICE**

High school students seeking advanced academic preparation typically evaluate four major pathways: Dual Enrollment/Concurrent Enrollment, Advanced Placement (AP), International Baccalaureate (IB), and Cambridge Advanced International Certificate of Education (AICE).

The fundamental distinction between these pathways lies in the mechanism used to earn [postsecondary credit](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf).

| Program Feature | College Credit Programs (Dual/Concurrent Enrollment) | Credit-by-Examination Programs (AP, IB, AICE) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary Credit Mechanism** | Semester-long course completion with a passing grade (typically C or higher)9. | Scoring high enough on a single [standardized end-of-year examination](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf). |
| **Transcript Issued Upon Completion** | Yes, an immediate official [college transcript](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf) is established. | No college transcript is issued until matriculation and score evaluation. |
| **Impact on College GPA** | Letter grade factors directly into the permanent [postsecondary GPA](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf). | Grade does not affect college GPA; awarded solely as test/prior learning credit. |
| **Assessment Structure** | Continuous [semester evaluation](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf) (quizzes, midterms, projects, finals). | One high-stakes standardized exam administered at the conclusion of the year. |
| **In-State Public Transfer Policy** | Guaranteed by [state statutes](https://www.fldoe.org/schools/higher-ed/fl-college-system/dual-enroll-transfer/) or systemwide articulation agreements. | Guaranteed based on state-mandated minimum cutoff exam scores. |
| **Out-of-State & Private Transferability** | Evaluated course-by-course based on [institutional syllabus review](https://transfer.vcu.edu/planning-your-transfer/passport-ucgs-courses/). | Widely recognized based on institutional exam score policies. |

### **Coursework Completion vs. High-Stakes Testing**

In dual enrollment and concurrent enrollment programs, credit is earned through continuous academic performance over an [entire college term](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf). If a student completes the course requirements and earns a passing grade, [college credit is granted](https://www.usf.edu/early-university-programs/dual-enrollment/requirements-and-deadlines.aspx).

In contrast, AP, IB, and Cambridge AICE classes are advanced high school courses where postsecondary credit is contingent upon performance on a single [end-of-year examination](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf). A student can earn an "A" in an AP class all year, but if they score a 2 on the AP exam, no college credit is awarded.

### **Transcript and Admission Evaluation**

Out-of-state public universities and highly selective private colleges often view these credits differently. Selective private institutions frequently grant credit for AP exam scores of 4 or 5 while refusing dual enrollment transfer credits if those courses were also used to satisfy high school graduation diploma requirements. Conversely, state public university systems are frequently mandated by state law to accept [in-state dual enrollment credits](https://www.fldoe.org/schools/higher-ed/fl-college-system/dual-enroll-transfer/).

## **Why Your State Matters**

A student's access to college credit options in high school is largely determined by their [state of residence](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/). Because public education governance and higher education funding are state responsibilities under the U.S. Constitution, state legislatures design their own postsecondary access frameworks.

Four major state-level policy factors shape a student's experience:

1. **Statutory Mandates**: States like Washington, Minnesota, Ohio, and Florida establish a statutory right for qualified students to enroll in college courses, requiring public school districts and state colleges to participate. Other states leave program adoption entirely to [voluntary local agreements](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/).
2. **State Funding Appropriations**: In [state-funded models](https://www.doe.virginia.gov/teaching-learning-assessment/advanced-learning/dual-enrollment/college-and-career-ready-virginia-ccrv), state appropriations directly reimburse colleges for high school student enrollment. In [district-funded models](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf), school districts pay college tuition out of local state-aid allocations. In student-paid models, families pay tuition out-of-pocket, often at [state-mandated discounted rates](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf).
3. **Higher Education System Integration**: States with centralized higher education structures (such as the [Virginia Community College System](https://www.doe.virginia.gov/teaching-learning-assessment/advanced-learning/dual-enrollment/college-and-career-ready-virginia-ccrv) or Florida College System) establish uniform course numbering and guaranteed transfer pathways across public institutions. States with autonomous university boards feature variable institutional transfer policies.
4. **Eligibility Rules**: State policies determine whether eligibility is based on a [statewide high school GPA threshold](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/), standardized placement tests (such as the PERT, ACCUPLACER, SAT, or ACT), or individual college admissions decisions.

## **The Most Important Question: Will the Credits Transfer?**

The most critical concept for students and families to master is that **earning college credit is not the same as earning credit that helps complete a college degree**.

When a student successfully completes a college course in high school, the college issuing the credit records it on an [official transcript](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf). However, when the student later enrolls as a degree-seeking undergraduate at a different college, the receiving university evaluates that transcript to determine how—and if—the [credits will be applied](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf).

```
TRANSCRIPT CREDIT EVALUATION PATHWAYS:  
College Transcript Submitted \-\Receiving College Evaluates Course  
  ├─\Direct Course Equivalency (Fulfills exact major or gen-ed requirement)  
  ├─\General Education Core Credit (Satisfies general education breadth block)  
  ├─\General Elective Credit (Counts toward total degree hours only)  
  └─\Credit Denied / Non-Transferable (Course rejected due to policy or syllabus gaps)

```

### **The Three Tiers of Transfer Acceptance**

* **Direct Course Equivalency**: The receiving university evaluates the dual enrollment course and determines it matches an exact course in its own catalog. *Example*: A student completes "ENG 101: Composition" at a community college. The receiving university accepts it as the direct equivalent of its own "ENGL 1101: Academic Writing," fully satisfying that graduation requirement.
* **General Education Core Credit**: The receiving university accepts the credit and applies it toward a broad general education distribution requirement, even if it does not match a specific course in its catalog. *Example*: A student completes "PSY 101: Intro to Psychology." The receiving college applies the 3 credits toward its "Social and Behavioral Sciences" general education core requirement.
* **General Elective Credit**: The receiving university accepts the credits toward the student's total degree credit count (e.g., counting 3 credits toward the 120 total credits needed to graduate), but the course does not satisfy any general education core or major requirement.

**Remember:** A [state transfer agreement](https://www.fldoe.org/schools/higher-ed/fl-college-system/dual-enroll-transfer/) can guarantee that credits transfer between public colleges within that state, but it does not mean an out-of-state or private college will apply those credits in the exact same way.

To streamline credit transfer, several state legislatures have created [guaranteed general education transfer packages](https://www.doe.virginia.gov/teaching-learning-assessment/advanced-learning/dual-enrollment/college-and-career-ready-virginia-ccrv):

* **Virginia’s Passport & Uniform Certificate of General Studies (UCGS)**: A 16-credit (Passport) or 31-credit (UCGS) course block guaranteed to transfer and satisfy lower-division general education requirements at all [public universities in Virginia](https://www.doe.virginia.gov/teaching-learning-assessment/advanced-learning/dual-enrollment/college-and-career-ready-virginia-ccrv).
* **Indiana College Core**: A 30-credit block of general education courses guaranteed to [transfer seamlessly](https://www.in.gov/che/academic-affairs/credit-transfer/) among all Indiana public postsecondary institutions.
* **MassTransfer Gen Ed Foundation**: A 34-credit block guaranteed to transfer across Massachusetts public community colleges, state universities, and [UMass campuses](https://www.rcc.mass.edu/enroll/steps-to-apply/dual-enrollment.html).

### Comparative Analysis: Strategic Models

#### 1. Funding Mechanisms

* **Fully State-Funded ($0 Out-of-Pocket):** Minnesota (PSEO), Ohio (CCP), Florida, and Virginia (CCRV pathways) mandate zero tuition, fees, or textbook costs for public school students.
* **Cap-Limited Funding:** Georgia caps state funding at **30 semester credits**, while Ohio caps funding at **30 credits per year** (120 credits max total).
* **Lottery & Subsidized Models:** Tennessee uses **state lottery revenue** to fund grants, while New York (SUNY) relies on discounted tuition rates negotiated between local school districts and regional colleges.
* **District-Paid Transfer:** Colorado requires the local school district to pay community college tuition directly using K-12 funding allocations.

#### 2. Credit Transferability & Statewide Articulation

* **Guaranteed Block Transfer:** **Texas** (42-credit Core Curriculum) and **Virginia** (16-credit Passport / 31-credit UCGS) use statutory transfer guarantees, ensuring credits earned in high school count directly toward general education requirements at any public in-state university.
* **Cooperative Articulation:** States like Pennsylvania rely on centralized transfer portals (PA TRAC) to navigate articulation agreements on a course-by-course or school-by-school basis.

#### 3. Delivery Models

* **Concurrent Enrollment (In High School):** Courses taught inside high schools by credentialed K-12 teachers (e.g., Minnesota CIS, Washington College in the High School, New York CUNY College Now).
* **Direct College Enrollment (On Campus / Online):** Students attend classes alongside traditional college students (e.g., Washington Running Start, Minnesota PSEO).
* **Early College High Schools (Extended Frameworks):** Programs like North Carolina’s CIHS, Colorado’s ASCENT, and Colorado’s TREP extend high school into a 5th year, allowing students to earn an Associate Degree or specialized credential at zero tuition cost before officially graduating.

### 6-Point Pre-Enrollment Decision Checklist

1. **Eligibility ("Can I take it?")**
Verify that you meet state and institutional grade-level mandates, unweighted GPA thresholds, and required placement test scores (SAT, ACT, PERT, or ACCUPLACER).
2. **Total Cost ("Can I afford it?")**
Determine whether tuition, mandatory campus fees, science lab material fees, digital access codes, textbooks, and transportation are fully covered or require out-of-pocket payment.
3. **Academic Workload ("Can I handle it?")**
Assess whether you are prepared for faster pacing, extensive independent reading, cumulative midterms/finals, and strict college-level grading standards.
4. **Transcription ("Will I receive college credit?")**
Confirm that passing the course generates an official college transcript with a permanent letter grade, rather than just high school honors credit.
5. **Transferability ("Will my future college accept it?")**
Check if your target postsecondary institutions recognize the credits through state articulation agreements, statewide transfer pathways, or official course equivalency databases.
6. **Degree Applicability ("Will it help me graduate?")**
Ensure the course fulfills a general education core requirement or major prerequisite, rather than accumulating as unallocated elective hours.

---

### Critical Pitfalls to Avoid

* **Accumulating Unaligned Electives:** Taking introductory courses outside required general education blocks or major tracks leads to excess credit hours that do not shorten time-to-degree.
* **Underestimating GPA Permanence:** Grades earned in dual enrollment classes permanently establish your official undergraduate transcript and cumulative college GPA, impacting future college admissions, scholarships, and financial aid eligibility.
* **Missing Postsecondary Drop Deadlines:** Colleges enforce hard withdrawal dates that differ from high school schedules; failing to formally drop a struggling course leads to a permanent "W" or failing grade ("F") on your transcript.
* **Overlooking Hidden Non-Tuition Costs:** Programs offering "free tuition" may still leave families responsible for lab equipment, course software subscriptions, campus parking permits, and physical textbooks.
* **Mismatches Between Transferability and Degree Applicability:** A university accepting a course for general transfer credit does not guarantee that credit fulfills specific departmental or major requirements for graduation.

## **How College Credit Fits Into Your High School Plan**

Integrating postsecondary coursework into a high school schedule requires balancing academic challenge with personal well-being. Earning college credit should support—not compromise—your overall educational journey.

**Before You Enroll:** Maintain a balanced schedule. Taking a full load of college courses while managing varsity athletics, extracurricular leadership, or part-time employment can lead to burnout. Always prioritize [earning high grades in fewer classes over struggling through an overcrowded course load](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf).

Save digital copies of every college course syllabus, course description, and official transcript. When you apply to four-year universities or request transfer credit evaluations down the road, having complete documentation makes [syllabus reviews easier if a course equivalency is questioned](https://transfer.vcu.edu/planning-your-transfer/passport-ucgs-courses/).

## **Frequently Asked Questions**

### **Can I earn college credit while I am in high school?**

Yes. Millions of high school students [earn college credit through state dual enrollment programs](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/), concurrent enrollment classes, early college high schools, and exam-based courses like AP, IB, or Cambridge AICE.

### **Is dual enrollment free?**

It depends on your [state and local school district policy](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/). States like Florida, Ohio, and Washington cover tuition for eligible public high school students, while other states require families to pay discounted tuition rates or [textbook fees](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf).

### **Is dual enrollment the same as dual credit?**

The terms are [frequently used interchangeably](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/). Technically, dual enrollment refers to the student being enrolled in two institutions at once, while dual credit refers to earning both high school diploma credit and college transcript credit for a single course.

### **What states offer free college-credit programs?**

States with robust tuition-free dual enrollment options include Florida, Washington (Running Start and College in the High School), Ohio (College Credit Plus), Minnesota (PSEO), [Virginia (CCRV)](https://www.doe.virginia.gov/teaching-learning-assessment/advanced-learning/dual-enrollment/college-and-career-ready-virginia-ccrv), Georgia, and North Carolina (Career & College Promise).

### **Can freshmen and sophomores participate?**

In some states, yes. Florida allows eligible students in [grades 6 through 12 to participate](https://www.fldoe.org/schools/higher-ed/fl-college-system/dual-enroll-transfer/). Other states limit general dual enrollment to juniors and seniors, or restrict 9th and 10th graders to specific high-school-based [concurrent enrollment or CTE pathways](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf).

### **Can homeschool and private school students participate?**

Yes, in many states. States like Florida, Minnesota, and Washington grant [homeschooled and non-public school students](https://www.fldoe.org/schools/higher-ed/fl-college-system/dual-enroll-transfer/) statutory access to state-funded dual enrollment, though administrative enrollment steps may differ.

### **Can I take college classes online?**

Yes. Most participating community colleges and state universities offer [online dual enrollment courses](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf) that can be completed from home or during a dedicated high school study hall.

### **Can I attend community college or a four-year university while in high school?**

Yes. Depending on local high school partnerships, students can take [courses on community college campuses](https://www.cwu.edu/academics/specialized-programs/college-high-school/_documents/ospi-faqs.pdf), at regional four-year state universities, or on high school grounds.

### **Can I earn an associate degree before graduating high school?**

Yes. Programs like Washington's Running Start, Minnesota's PSEO, Michigan's Early Middle College, and Early College High Schools allow dedicated students to complete an [Associate of Arts or Associate of Science degree](https://nccareers.org/career-college-promise?lang=ar) alongside their diploma.

### **Do dual enrollment grades go on a college transcript?**

Yes. Enrolling in a college credit course creates an [official, permanent postsecondary transcript](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf). Grades earned factor directly into your [college GPA](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf).

### **Will my dual enrollment credits transfer?**

In-state public colleges and universities usually accept credits under [statewide articulation agreements](https://www.fldoe.org/schools/higher-ed/fl-college-system/dual-enroll-transfer/). Out-of-state and selective private universities evaluate courses individually based on syllabus content and [individual transfer policies](https://dualcreditplaybook.nyc/web/overview).

### **Is dual enrollment harder than AP?**

Not necessarily, but the grading structure is different. AP course credit depends entirely on one end-of-year [standardized exam score](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-08/pl-dual-credit.pdf). Dual enrollment courses evaluate [semester-long performance](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/rs-faqs-august-2023.pdf) through college exams, papers, and projects.

### **What happens if I fail or drop a dual enrollment class?**

Failing a dual enrollment class results in an "F" on both your high school and official college transcripts, lowering your GPA and potentially impacting [future federal financial aid eligibility](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf). Dropping past official college deadlines creates a [permanent "W" or "F"](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf) on your postsecondary record.

## **Unlocking the Real Benefits of Early College Credit**

College credit programs in high school offer powerful opportunities to build academic confidence, experience college expectations, and [reduce the overall cost of a higher education degree](https://www.ecs.org/50-state-comparison-dual-concurrent-enrollment-policies/). However, there is no single nationwide framework or one-size-fits-all approach.

The goal of early college planning is not simply collecting as many college credits as possible. The real objective is earning credits that are [affordable, transferable, degree-applicable, and worth the academic effort](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1531768152/gulllakecsorg/grkgdvjc3xjhssemxl5n/GuidelinesforDualEnrollment.pdf). Start by examining what your state and local high school offer, understand the academic and financial rules, and ensure that every course you take [aligns with your long-term educational plans](https://www.fldoe.org/schools/higher-ed/fl-college-system/dual-enroll-transfer/).
