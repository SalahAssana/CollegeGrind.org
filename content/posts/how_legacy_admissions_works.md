In the highly competitive arena of selective college admissions, few topics provoke as much confusion, anxiety, and intense debate as legacy admissions. For many students and families, the process of applying to elite universities feels like trying to crack a coded lock, and legacy status is frequently viewed as either a master key or an insurmountable barrier.

Public perception of this practice tends to polarize into two incorrect extremes. The first extreme is the belief that legacy status represents an absolute guarantee of admission—a golden ticket that easily bypasses academic, extracurricular, and personal standards, allowing underqualified applicants to claim spots ahead of vastly more accomplished peers. The second extreme is the belief that legacy preferences are a relic of the past, a minor detail on the Common Application that has no real impact on the outcomes of non-legacy applicants.

The reality of legacy admissions is far more complex and highly variable. Legacy admission is real and can provide a meaningful advantage at specific institutions, but it is rarely an automatic admit for academically underqualified students. More commonly, it acts as a [meaningful “plus factor” or tiebreaker among already highly competitive applicants](https://orieladmissions.com/legacy-admissions-complete-guide/)—and its importance varies dramatically from school to school. To understand how legacy admissions truly affects an applicant’s chances, one must move past the headlines and examine the historical origins, institutional motivations, and actual data that define modern university admissions.

## **What Legacy Admissions Actually Means**

To analyze the impact of legacy admissions, one must first establish a clear definition of what constitutes a "legacy applicant." A common misconception is that any family connection to a university qualifies a student for preferential treatment. In practice, there is no universal, standardized definition of legacy; instead, it is a [highly customized institutional category that varies significantly across different colleges and universities](https://orieladmissions.com/legacy-admissions-complete-guide/).

Admissions offices generally categorize legacy ties based on the closeness of the familial relationship and the specific division of the university the relative attended. The hierarchy of legacy preferences is structured as follows:

* **Primary (Undergraduate) Legacy:** This is the strongest and most universally recognized legacy tie. It applies when an applicant’s parent or step-parent earned an undergraduate degree from the institution. Admissions offices prioritize undergraduate ties because undergraduate alumni tend to have the deepest emotional and financial connection to the university.
* **Graduate or Professional School Legacy:** This applies when a parent attended a graduate program (such as the law, business, or medical school) of a university. Many institutions treat graduate legacy status with less weight than undergraduate status, while some do not consider it a legacy factor at all.
* **Grandparent Legacy:** Some universities extend legacy consideration to grandchildren of alumni. However, the weight given to grandparent ties is almost always significantly lower than that given to parent ties, serving more as a point of interest rather than a formal "tip" in the evaluation process.
* **Sibling Legacy:** A sibling tie is rarely treated as a standard legacy factor in the same way a parental tie is. While having a sibling attend a school shows high institutional interest, many schools do not award the same structural advantage for siblings, although sibling connections may be evaluated favorably during [early admissions rounds](https://talk.collegeconfidential.com/t/legacy-percentage/1822655).
* **Extended Family Legacy:** Relatives such as aunts, uncles, cousins, nieces, or nephews are [almost never factored into the legacy admissions equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_preferences). While applicants can list these relatives on the Common Application, admissions offices generally disregard them when [determining legacy preferences](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/legacy-in-college-admissions/).

The table below illustrates how legacy definitions differ across select institutions, highlighting that a relationship that is highly valued at one university may be completely disregarded at another:

| Institution | Considers Parent Undergraduate? | Considers Parent Graduate? | Considers Grandparent? | Considers Sibling? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Princeton University** | [Yes](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/legacy-in-college-admissions/) | No | No | No |
| **University of Notre Dame** | [Yes](https://www.koppelmangroup.com/blog/2025/4/8/notre-dame-legacy-policies-and-guide) | No | No | No |
| **Duke University** | Yes | [Yes](https://talk.collegeconfidential.com/t/legacy-percentage/1822655) | No | Yes (Early Decision Only) |
| **University of Pennsylvania** | [Yes](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/) | No | No | No |

Furthermore, legacy status must be distinguished from "[donor preference](https://orieladmissions.com/legacy-admissions-complete-guide/)" or "development admissions". While the public often conflates the two, standard legacy preference applies to the children of ordinary alumni who may make modest annual donations or simply participate in local alumni chapters. Development cases involve the children of major philanthropic benefactors or prospective high-net-worth donors. These files are tracked separately (frequently on a university's "Dean's Interest List") and are evaluated under a different set of institutional considerations than standard legacy applications.

## **Why Colleges Use Legacy Admissions**

To understand why legacy admissions persists at many of the nation’s most selective private institutions, it is necessary to examine the practice through an institutional lens rather than a moral or political one. Universities are complex, multi-generational organizations that operate under competing pressures, including financial sustainability, community cohesion, and academic prestige. Admissions offices utilize legacy preferences to advance several distinct institutional objectives.

### **Historical Origins of Legacy Preferences**

The practice of legacy admissions did not emerge by accident; it was developed during a specific, highly exclusionary period in American higher education. In the early twentieth century, elite universities—specifically Harvard, Yale, and Princeton (often referred to as the "Big Three")—admitted applicants [primarily based on performance on standard entrance examinations](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/7312037d-36d4-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/download). Under this academic system, any student who passed the exams was admitted, and schools did not cap enrollment or maintain formal admissions offices.

During the 1910s and 1920s, this exam-based system led to a rapid demographic shift. High numbers of immigrant students, particularly eastern European Jewish students, began passing the entrance exams and enrolling in elite colleges in large numbers. At Columbia University, the freshman class became 40 percent Jewish; at Harvard, the proportion of Jewish students rose to nearly 30 percent.

Worried that wealthy Protestant families would abandon these campuses—a phenomenon administrators termed "WASP flight"—and seeking to maintain the social composition of their student bodies, university leaders sought ways to limit Jewish enrollment without explicitly setting religious quotas, which would have triggered public backlash. Under the leadership of Columbia’s admissions office and Harvard’s President Abbott Lawrence Lowell, universities restructured the admissions process to be highly discretionary and holistic.

They capped class sizes, established admissions offices, and introduced non-academic criteria such as personal essays, letters of recommendation from high school headmasters, interviews, and "character" evaluations. To ensure that the children of their predominantly Protestant, wealthy alumni continued to gain admission regardless of test scores, universities officially introduced preferences for geographic diversity, athletic ability, and alumni lineage (the legacy preference). This shift successfully altered the definition of "merit" away from pure scholarship to subjective "well-roundedness," establishing the modern framework of holistic admissions.

### **Modern Institutional Incentives**

While the origins of legacy admissions are rooted in early twentieth-century social preservation, the survival of the practice into the twenty-first century is driven by powerful, pragmatic institutional incentives. Private universities defend the practice because they believe it supports their operational and financial health in several key ways:

* **Alumni Giving and Endowment Support:** Highly selective private colleges rely heavily on their endowments to fund undergraduate financial aid, research facilities, and faculty salaries. Universities argue that [admitting legacy students fosters multi-generational family loyalty](https://www.myivylink.com/blog/wesleyan-waves-goodbye-to-legacy-admissions), which directly translates into sustained philanthropic giving. While critics point to studies showing that ending legacy admissions does not necessarily decrease aggregate alumni donations, [university advancement offices remain deeply fearful](https://www.commandeducation.com/amherst-ends-legacy-admissions/) of any policy that might alienate their most reliable donor bases.
* **Yield Protection and Enrollment Modeling:** A university's "yield rate"—the percentage of admitted students who choose to enroll—is a critical metric. High yield rates make enrollment numbers [highly predictable and signal a school's desirability](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-widespread-is-the-practice-of-giving-special-consideration-to-relatives-of-alumni-in-admissions/), which heavily influences national rankings. Legacy students have exceptionally high yield rates; they are intimately familiar with the campus culture, traditions, and reputation. For example, at the [University of Notre Dame, approximately 75% of admitted legacy students choose to enroll](https://www.koppelmangroup.com/blog/2025/4/8/notre-dame-legacy-policies-and-guide), compared to a 50% yield rate for non-legacy admits. Admitting legacies allows enrollment managers to secure a highly predictable block of matriculants early in the cycle.
* **Community, Tradition, and Culture:** Universities view themselves as distinct communities with unique traditions and values. Proponents of legacy preferences argue that [multi-generational attendance preserves institutional memory](https://www.tynker.com/blog/legacy-college-admissions/) and campus culture. Legacy students often arrive on campus already deeply invested in the university's identity, which administrators believe helps build a highly cohesive and active student body.

## **How Legacy Admissions Works in Practice**

The persistent public debate over legacy admissions is fueled by a lack of transparency regarding how admissions offices actually evaluate and weigh legacy status during file review. Many assume that legacy files are fast-tracked or evaluated under entirely different standards, but the physical reality of the holistic review process is much more nuanced.

### **The Holistic Review Process and Academic Floors**

In selective college admissions, applications are evaluated through a holistic review process, which means that every component of a student's file—grades, course rigor, standardized test scores, essays, recommendations, extracurricular activities, and personal context—is considered in relation to the whole.

Within this framework, legacy status is not a primary credential; it is a contextual factor, or "tag," that is noted on the file. Admissions officers consistently emphasize that **legacy status does not lower the academic floor** of an institution. If an applicant’s grades and test scores place them well below the competitive range for a school, their legacy status will not rescue their application. Highly selective private universities receive far more applications from [academically qualified legacies than they have space to admit](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/legacy-in-college-admissions/), meaning that thousands of students with legacy status and near-perfect academic profiles are rejected every year.

An applicant's academic admissibility can be conceptualized through a simplified probability model. Let P(Admit) represent the probability of admission, modeled as a function of academic qualifications (A), extracurricular credentials (E), and institutional priorities (I):

```
P(Admit) = f(A,E,I)

```

For an applicant where A falls below the university's strict minimum threshold (A_min), the probability of admission is zero, regardless of the value of I (which includes legacy status):

```
If A < A_min, then P(Admit) = 0

```

However, once an applicant meets or exceeds the academic floor (A > A_min), [legacy status enters the equation as a powerful multiplier](https://orieladmissions.com/legacy-admissions-complete-guide/) within the institutional priorities vector (I).

### **Legacy as a "Plus Factor" or Tiebreaker**

The most accurate way to understand legacy status is as a highly influential "plus factor" or tiebreaker. In a hypothetical admissions committee, imagine two applicants, Student A and Student B, who are applying to an elite private university with a 5% overall acceptance rate.

| Evaluation Metric | Student A (Non-Legacy) | Student B (Legacy) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unweighted GPA** | 3.96 | 3.94 |
| **ACT Score** | 35 | 34 |
| **Course Rigor** | 12 AP/IB courses (Maximum available) | 11 AP/IB courses (Maximum available) |
| **Extracurriculars** | Varsity Track Captain, Debate President | Mock Trial Captain, Orchestral Violinist |
| **Recommendation Letters** | Highly enthusiastic; praise academic curiosity | Exceptionally strong; highlight leadership |
| **Legacy Status** | None | Parent graduated with B.A. from the school |

In this scenario, both students are demonstrably capable of excelling academically at the university. Because the school must reject the vast majority of its qualified applicant pool to meet its class size targets, the admissions committee cannot admit both. This is where [legacy status operates as a decisive weight on the scale](https://apicciano.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2021/10/08/johns-hopkins-president-ronald-j-daniel-abolish-legacy-admissions-now/). Because Student B has a parent who attended the school, the university knows that Student B has a high probability of enrolling if accepted (protecting yield) and that their family is highly likely to remain engaged, supportive donors. Under the rules of holistic review, Student B is highly likely to be admitted over Student A, not because Student A is unqualified, but because Student B offers identical academic promise plus a valuable institutional tie.

### **Distinguishing Legacy from Development and Donor Admissions**

It is critical to separate standard legacy preference from development or donor-advised admissions. In a typical admissions cycle, legacy applications are processed within the [standard regional reading pools](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/). Admissions officers note the legacy tag, but the evaluation remains anchored in the standard committee process.

Development admits, by contrast, involve applicants whose families have a documented history of major philanthropic giving or possess the clear capacity to make transformative donations (such as funding a new research center, endowed chair, or scholarship fund). These applicants are tracked through specialized institutional lists, often in close communication with the university's development and fundraising offices.

Data from the landmark *Students for Fair Admissions (SFFA) v. Harvard* lawsuit revealed that while a standard legacy connection provides a substantial boost, a spot on the "Dean's Interest List" (which includes donor-linked applicants) provides an even larger advantage. Specifically, an applicant with a standard legacy tie saw a five-fold increase in their probability of admission compared to an identical non-legacy applicant, while a donor-linked applicant on the Dean's Interest List saw a [more than seven-fold increase](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26316/w26316.pdf).

## **When and Where Legacy Matters Most—and Least**

The influence of legacy status is not uniform across the landscape of American higher education. An applicant's ability to leverage an alumni connection depends entirely on the type of institution, its geographic location, and the specific admissions round in which the student applies.

### **Where Legacy Matters Most**

Legacy preferences are most concentrated and influential at **highly selective, private institutions**. This category includes:

* **Ivy League and Ivy Plus Universities:** Schools like Harvard, Yale, Princeton, Columbia, Penn, Dartmouth, Duke, and Stanford have historically maintained deep traditions of legacy evaluation. These schools possess multi-billion-dollar endowments and active, [highly organized global alumni networks](https://www.tynker.com/blog/legacy-college-admissions/) that they seek to preserve and cultivate.
* **Elite Liberal Arts Colleges:** Highly selective liberal arts colleges (such as Williams, Bowdoin, and historically Amherst and Wesleyan) place an immense emphasis on close-knit community culture and high alumni participation. Because their classes are exceptionally small (often fewer than 500 students per year), a legacy connection has historically served as a critical indicator of cultural fit.
* **Institutions with Strong Athletic and Alumni Cultures:** Universities with deep-seated institutional pride, such as Notre Dame, rely extensively on [multi-generational families to sustain their campus identity](https://www.koppelmangroup.com/blog/2025/4/8/notre-dame-legacy-policies-and-guide) and financial health.

### **Where Legacy Matters Least**

Conversely, legacy status is a negligible or non-existent factor at several other types of institutions:

* **Public Universities:** The vast majority of public flagship universities do not consider legacy status in undergraduate admissions, or they award only minor consideration. Because public universities are funded by state taxpayers and governed by public boards, [awarding admissions advantages based on ancestry is politically and legally difficult to justify](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-widespread-is-the-practice-of-giving-special-consideration-to-relatives-of-alumni-in-admissions/).
* **Highly Formulaic Admissions Systems:** Institutions that rely primarily on formulaic index scores (combining GPA and standardized test scores) to determine admissibility have little room for subjective, holistic "tags" like legacy status.
* **Merit-First Institutions:** Certain elite private universities have long established institutional policies that explicitly forbid the consideration of legacy status. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) have never considered legacy status, choosing instead to focus entirely on [academic and talent-based metrics](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/legacy-in-college-admissions/).

### **The Early Decision (ED) and Restrictive Early Action (REA) Interaction**

One of the most important strategic insights for families is that **the legacy advantage is highly dependent on the admissions round**. At many elite private institutions, legacy preference is active almost exclusively during the Early Decision (ED) or Restrictive Early Action (REA) rounds.

Applying Early Decision is a binding commitment: if admitted, the student must enroll and withdraw all other applications. Because ED [guarantees a 100% yield for those admitted](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/who-really-benefits-from-applying-early/), universities are highly motivated to accept competitive students in this round.

At universities like the University of Pennsylvania, Cornell, and Columbia, legacy applicants are openly encouraged to apply during the binding Early Decision round to receive legacy consideration. Admissions offices at these schools interpret a legacy applicant’s decision to apply in the Regular Decision round as a signal that the student is prioritizing other Ivy League institutions, thereby neutralizing any legacy advantage they might have possessed.

By concentrating legacy admits in the Early Decision round, universities achieve a double benefit: they satisfy their alumni base, secure a highly qualified cohort of matriculants, [protect their yield rate](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/), and preserve the Regular Decision round to shape and diversify the remainder of the incoming class.

## **Extensive School-by-School Examples**

To understand the practical application of legacy policies, it is necessary to examine specific institutions. The way universities describe, track, and execute their legacy preferences varies enormously.

### **Schools That Strongly Consider Legacy**

#### **Harvard University**

Harvard’s admissions practices were thoroughly exposed during the [*Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* Supreme Court case](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26316/w26316.pdf), providing unprecedented data on the scale of legacy preferences. According to data released during the lawsuit, Harvard’s overall acceptance rate hovered around 4.5% to 5%, but the acceptance rate for legacy applicants (defined as having a parent who attended Harvard undergraduate college) was [approximately 34%—nearly six times the rate of non-legacy applicants](https://dho.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/bostonreview.net-What-Statistics-Cant-Tell-Us-in-the-Fight-over-Affirmative-Action-at-Harvard.pdf).

The data revealed that legacy applicants are, on average, academically stronger than the general applicant pool. However, the average admitted legacy student scored slightly lower on Harvard's internal academic ratings than the average admitted non-legacy student, indicating that the legacy status acted as a powerful compensatory boost.

Economist Peter Arcidiacono's modeling demonstrated that roughly three-quarters (75%) of white legacy, athlete, and donor-related admits would have been rejected if they had been evaluated as standard, non-hooked white applicants. Legacy admits accounted for [roughly 22% of all admitted white students at Harvard](https://dho.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/bostonreview.net-What-Statistics-Cant-Tell-Us-in-the-Fight-over-Affirmative-Action-at-Harvard.pdf), compared to just 7% of admitted Asian American students, illustrating the demographic disparities associated with the policy.

#### **University of Pennsylvania (Penn)**

Penn has historically maintained one of the most explicit legacy policies in the Ivy League. According to [its admissions guidelines](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/), legacy preference is applied almost exclusively during the binding Early Decision round.

Penn’s [Common Data Set explicitly rates "alumni relation" as an important non-academic factor](https://admitreport.com/common-data-set) in its holistic evaluation. The university's enrollment management strategy relies on legacy applicants to help fill approximately 50% of the incoming class through the Early Decision round. Legacy applicants who bypass the ED round and apply to Penn during the Regular Decision round are evaluated without the legacy "tip".

#### **Cornell University**

Similar to Penn, Cornell University highly values alumni connections but restricts the primary advantage of legacy status to the Early Decision round. Cornell’s admissions office uses the binding commitment of ED to verify that a legacy applicant is genuinely committed to attending. Applying Regular Decision as a legacy applicant at Cornell is [frequently interpreted as a signal](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/) that the student is prioritizing other Ivy League institutions, thereby neutralizing their alumni advantage.

#### **Yale and Princeton Universities**

Both Yale and Princeton utilize restrictive, non-binding early application programs (Single-Choice Early Action or Restrictive Early Action). SCEA/REA prevents applicants from applying early to other private universities but does not legally bind them to enroll if admitted.

Both institutions explicitly consider legacy status (strictly defined as parents) as a ["plus factor" in their holistic reviews](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/legacy-in-college-admissions/). At both schools, the proportion of legacy students in each first-year class has historically hovered around 10% to 12%. Applying during the SCEA/REA round is considered the optimal way for legacy candidates to leverage their family ties at these institutions.

#### **Columbia University**

Columbia University describes legacy status as a "positive factor" in its holistic evaluation. However, Columbia has faced intense student and faculty pressure regarding its legacy policies. Columbia's admissions model is highly sensitive to yield protection, meaning that the binding nature of its Early Decision program is heavily leveraged. To receive [any structural benefit from legacy status at Columbia](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/), applicants are practically required to apply during the ED round.

#### **Dartmouth College**

As a smaller, highly community-centric Ivy League institution, Dartmouth College ("The Big Green") places an exceptionally high premium on institutional fit and multi-generational loyalty. Dartmouth strictly limits its legacy definition to parents and [applies a significant preference](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/) during its Early Decision round. Because of Dartmouth's remote location in Hanover, New Hampshire, the admissions office highly values applicants who have grown up with a deep familiarity and appreciation of the school's unique, outdoors-oriented campus culture.

#### **University of Notre Dame**

Notre Dame represents the absolute peak of legacy influence among top-tier American universities. Notre Dame’s legacy policy is an open and celebrated component of its institutional identity, serving as a "thank you" to the multi-generational Catholic families that support the university.

At Notre Dame, legacy status is [strictly limited to parents](https://www.koppelmangroup.com/blog/2025/4/8/notre-dame-legacy-policies-and-guide) (not grandparents or siblings). Remarkably, up to 20% to 25% of each enrolling first-year class is comprised of legacy students—nearly double or triple the percentage found at Ivy League institutions.

The acceptance rate for legacy applicants at Notre Dame has historically hovered around 45%, compared to an overall acceptance rate that has [plummeted to a record-low 9%](https://talk.collegeconfidential.com/t/nd-legacy/1930633). Notre Dame’s admissions office processes legacy files through a unique protocol: if a competitive legacy applicant is not admitted during the Restrictive Early Action round, they are almost always deferred to the Regular Decision round for a "second look" rather than being rejected outright.

#### **Duke University**

Duke University considers legacy status (defined to include undergraduate, graduate, and professional alumni parent ties) as a factor in its holistic review, particularly during its binding Early Decision round.

Data from admissions presentations by long-time Dean of Undergraduate Admissions Christoph Guttentag indicated that legacy applicants at Duke are admitted at significantly higher rates than the general population. For example, in a historical cycle, the overall ED acceptance rate was 23%, while the legacy ED acceptance rate was 35%; in the Regular Decision round, the overall acceptance rate was 10%, while the legacy RD acceptance rate was 16%. Legacies typically comprise [10% to 15% of Duke's incoming classes](https://talk.collegeconfidential.com/t/legacy-percentage/1822655).

#### **Georgetown University**

Georgetown University has historically maintained a unique stance on legacy admissions. Unlike other top-15 institutions, Georgetown does not use a binding Early Decision program, offering only a [non-binding Early Action (EA) option](https://orieladmissions.com/georgetown-acceptance-rate/).

Crucially, Georgetown does not consider legacy status at all during the Early Action round, reserving its evaluation of alumni ties strictly for the Regular Decision round. Legacies historically account for [approximately 9% of Georgetown’s admitted classes](https://thehoya.com/features/georgetown-admits-12-of-applicants-to-undergraduate-class-of-2028-as-students-call-for-end-to-legacy-admissions/).

### **Schools That Ended Legacy Preferences**

The legal and political landscape of college admissions shifted dramatically following the June 2023 Supreme Court decision in *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard*, which declared race-conscious admissions unconstitutional.

In the wake of this ruling, critics argued that maintaining admissions preferences for a predominantly white, wealthy alumni base was indefensible if universities could no longer utilize affirmative action to support underrepresented students of color. This catalyzed a [wave of voluntary policy changes](https://www.myivylink.com/blog/wesleyan-waves-goodbye-to-legacy-admissions) and state-level legislative bans.

The table below outlines the timeline of select institutions that ended legacy admissions, showing how the trend accelerated after 2023:

| Institution | Year Legacy Preference Ended | Primary Motivation / Stated Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Texas A&M University** | 2004 | [Banned race-conscious admissions; sought to maintain a strict meritocracy](https://images-production.authory.com/RuthTerry/4d69c0e8-8000-4c51-8d6b-c827e4b71431/91dc7df0-235a-11ef-b7bc-ed98e5b764c6.pdf) |
| **Johns Hopkins University** | 2014 | [Sought to eliminate hereditary privilege and increase socioeconomic diversity](https://apicciano.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2021/10/08/johns-hopkins-president-ronald-j-daniel-abolish-legacy-admissions-now/) |
| **Amherst College** | 2021 | [Desired to expand access to low- and middle-income students](https://www.amherst.edu/news/news_releases/2021/10-2021/amherst-college-to-end-legacy-preference-and-expand-financial-aid-investment-to-71-million/faq-expanded-financial-aid-and-elimination-of-legacy-preference) |
| **Carnegie Mellon University** | 2023 | [Aligned admissions policies with equity goals post-SFFA Supreme Court decision](https://www.bestcolleges.com/research/legacy-admissions-bans/) |
| **Wesleyan University** | 2023 | [Avoided institutional hypocrisy following the elimination of affirmative action](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-widespread-is-the-practice-of-giving-special-consideration-to-relatives-of-alumni-in-admissions/) |
| **University of Virginia (UVA)** | 2024 | [Complied with a unanimous bipartisan state law banning legacy preferences](https://uvamagazine.org/articles/new_law_bans_legacy_preferences) |

#### **Johns Hopkins University (JHU)**

Johns Hopkins is widely recognized as the pioneer of the modern anti-legacy movement. Under the leadership of President Ronald J. Daniels, the university quietly [eliminated legacy preferences in 2014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legacy_preferences). JHU chose to keep the decision quiet initially to observe and document its direct impact on the student body before announcing it publicly in 2019.

The results of JHU's policy shift were dramatic and structurally measurable:

* **Socioeconomic Diversity:** In 2009, legacy students made up 12.5% of the first-year class, while Pell Grant-eligible students (typically from families earning under $65,000) made up only 9%. By 2020, legacy representation fell to zero (no active preference was awarded, though some legacies still gained admission on pure merit, making up ~3.5% to 4.2% of the class), while [Pell-eligible enrollment surged to over 19% to 20.5%](https://apicciano.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2021/10/08/johns-hopkins-president-ronald-j-daniel-abolish-legacy-admissions-now/).
* **First-Generation Students:** The percentage of students who were the first in their families to attend college [more than doubled, rising from 7% in 2009 to 16% in 2020](https://apicciano.commons.gc.cuny.edu/2021/10/08/johns-hopkins-president-ronald-j-daniel-abolish-legacy-admissions-now/).
* **Academic Quality:** JHU admissions offices reported that the academic credentials (GPA and standardized test scores) of incoming classes continued to rise steadily throughout the post-legacy era, debunking the myth that [eliminating legacy preferences would harm academic excellence](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/elite-college-dropped-legacy-admissions-time-higher-education-institutions/).
* **Fundraising Impact:** Critically, JHU [experienced no drop in alumni giving or fundraising capability](https://www.commandeducation.com/amherst-ends-legacy-admissions/) after ending the policy, supported by massive philanthropic gifts and a highly engaged alumni network.

#### **Amherst College**

Amherst College, one of the nation's most prestigious liberal arts institutions, ended its century-old legacy preference policy in October 2021. Amherst paired this announcement with a [massive expansion of its undergraduate financial aid budget to $71 million annually](https://www.amherst.edu/news/news_releases/2021/10-2021/amherst-college-to-end-legacy-preference-and-expand-financial-aid-investment-to-71-million), explicitly aiming to recruit more low- and middle-income students.

Prior to the policy change, legacy students represented approximately 11% of each entering class. Amherst President Biddy Martin stated that ending legacy preference was necessary to "create as much opportunity for as many academically talented young people as possible, regardless of financial background or legacy status".

By removing the legacy "bump," Amherst flattened the admissions playing field, allowing its admissions team to allocate seats to highly competitive first-generation and low-income applicants who were [previously squeezed out in the final committee rounds](https://amherststudent.com/article/college-ends-legacy-admissions-expands-financial-aid-community-reacts/).

#### **Wesleyan University**

Wesleyan University formally abolished its legacy preference policy in July 2023, just weeks after the Supreme Court's affirmative action ruling. Wesleyan President Michael S. Roth explained that while legacy status had played a relatively "negligible role" in admissions for years—accounting for roughly 4% to 5% of the class—formally ending the practice was crucial to [eliminate a "sign of unfairness to the outside world"](https://www.myivylink.com/blog/wesleyan-waves-goodbye-to-legacy-admissions) and avoid institutional hypocrisy.

To offset the policy change, Wesleyan announced a [comprehensive recruitment strategy](https://www.wesleyan.edu/about/news/2023/07/wesleyan-university-to-end-legacy-admission.html) that included creating scholarships for students from Africa, increasing veteran recruiting pipelines, and enhancing community college transfer pathways. Roth noted that the response from Wesleyan alumni was "extremely supportive," indicating that modern alumni increasingly prioritize institutional fairness over structural advantages for their own children.

#### **Texas A&M University**

In 2004, Texas A&M University represented one of the earliest public flagship universities to eliminate legacy preferences. Under the leadership of then-President Robert Gates, the university [ended its "legacy point system,"](https://images-production.authory.com/RuthTerry/4d69c0e8-8000-4c51-8d6b-c827e4b71431/91dc7df0-235a-11ef-b7bc-ed98e5b764c6.pdf) which had previously awarded legacy applicants a four-point bonus on a 100-point admissions scale.

The policy shift occurred after Texas A&M chose to stop using race-conscious admissions. Gates argued that to maintain a consistent commitment to pure meritocracy, the university [could not justify maintaining ancestry-based preferences](https://thebatt.com/news/legacy-admissions-arent-merit-based/) while removing race-conscious ones.

#### **University of Virginia (UVA)**

The state of Virginia enacted a sweeping bipartisan law in March 2024 that prohibited all public higher education institutions in the commonwealth from [considering legacy or donor relations in admissions](https://uvamagazine.org/articles/new_law_bans_legacy_preferences). UVA immediately complied, obscuring legacy and racial demographic data from its admissions readers during the 2023-2024 application cycle, even before the law officially took effect.

For the Class of 2028 (the first class admitted under the post-legacy, post-affirmative action regime), UVA reported highly instructive outcomes:

* **Legacy Demographic Shift:** The percentage of legacy students in the first-year class dropped from 14.7% in the Class of 2027 to 12.9% in the Class of 2028. While this was a decrease, it demonstrated that highly qualified children of alumni still successfully [gained admission on their own merits](https://apnews.com/article/wesleyan-legacy-admissions-21f7781a961bd784e14dd7752a79d860).
* **Minimal Racial Demographic Fluctuations:** Unlike several of its private peer institutions (such as MIT, which saw an 8% drop in Black enrollment), UVA managed to maintain relatively stable racial demographic proportions. Underrepresented students of color remained well-represented. UVA’s Vice Provost for Enrollment, Stephen Farmer, [credited this stability to aggressive, pre-emptive recruitment initiatives](https://uvamagazine.org/articles/the_class_of_2028_by_the_numbers), such as the "All Virginia" project, which actively targeted low-income high schools throughout the state.
* **First-Generation and Pell Growth:** The share of first-generation college students [rose from 17.5% to approximately 19% of the incoming class](https://uvamagazine.org/articles/the_class_of_2028_by_the_numbers), showing a direct shift in seat allocation toward socioeconomically diverse candidates.

### **The Enforcement Mechanisms Behind the Legacy Bans**

The legislative push against legacy preferences relies on two entirely different legal and financial strategies to ensure colleges comply.

#### **California (Assembly Bill 1780): The "Name and Shame" Strategy**

California’s ban applies to both public and private institutions, directly impacting elite private universities like Stanford and USC. However, its enforcement mechanism changed significantly during the legislative process:

* **The Original Threat:** The initial draft of the bill carried a severe financial penalty: any private university caught violating the legacy ban would lose its state funding through the **Cal Grant financial aid program**—a penalty worth millions of dollars.
* **The Final Law:** Intense lobbying from private university coalitions successfully removed the financial penalty before the bill passed. Under the final law, California relies on **data reporting and public transparency**.
* **The Penalty:** There are no direct financial fines. Instead, institutions must report their legacy enrollment data to the state legislature. The State Attorney General is empowered to take legal action against non-compliant schools, and universities that violate the ban are essentially placed on an official, public "naughty list" managed by the state. Critics argue this lack of financial teeth might tempt some highly wealthy private schools to find loopholes in how they evaluate "institutional fit."

#### **Virginia: Total Structural Data Obscurity**

Virginia took a much cleaner, more direct approach, but restricted its ban strictly to **public universities** (such as UVA, Virginia Tech, and William & Mary). Because these are state-run institutions, compliance is integrated directly into university operations:

* **The Enforcement:** Instead of checking for violations after admissions decisions are made, Virginia schools ensure compliance by **completely blocking the data** from reaching the people making the decisions.
* **The Process:** During the admissions cycle, application platforms (like the Common App) still collect family educational history for demographic tracking, but this data is digitally redacted or hidden from admissions readers. A regional dean reading an application physically cannot see whether a student's parent attended the school, eliminating the possibility of a legacy "tip" during committee debates.

> **The Big Picture:** Public universities (like those in Virginia) must comply with state mandates as a matter of direct operational law. Private universities (like those in California) face a trickier environment where the law bans the practice, but the enforcement relies primarily on public transparency and the threat of state legal audits rather than immediate financial defunding.

## **FAQ Section**

### **What is a legacy applicant?**

A legacy applicant is a college applicant who has a familial relationship with an alumnus of the institution to which they are applying. The [precise definition of "legacy" varies enormously by school](https://ingeniusprep.com/blog/legacy-in-college-admissions/): some institutions only consider parental undergraduate degrees, while others extend the classification to grandparents, siblings, or graduate-school alumni.

### **Does legacy admission help?**

Yes, legacy status provides a meaningful advantage in the admissions process, but it functions primarily as a "plus factor" or tiebreaker among already highly qualified candidates. It [does not act as a rescue mechanism for a weak application](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/) or guarantee admission to an academically underqualified student.

### **Do colleges care about legacy?**

The level of institutional concern varies dramatically. Highly selective private universities and elite liberal arts colleges tend to care about legacy status due to financial, community, and [yield-protection motivations](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-widespread-is-the-practice-of-giving-special-consideration-to-relatives-of-alumni-in-admissions/). Conversely, most public universities and formulaic merit-focused schools place little or no weight on alumni relations.

### **Does legacy help in Early Decision?**

Yes. The admissions advantage for legacy status is heavily concentrated in early admissions rounds, such as Early Decision (ED) or Restrictive Early Action (REA). [Applying early signals a binding commitment and high yield probability](https://www.topdogcollege.com/articles/legacy-admissions-policies-at-the-top-50-us-colleges-what-you-need-to-know/), which aligns with institutional enrollment management goals.

### **What is the difference between donor admissions vs legacy admissions?**

Ordinary legacy admissions applies to the descendants of general alumni, who may make small donations or participate in school events. [Donor admissions (development cases) involve applicants whose families have made or are poised to make transformative, multi-million-dollar philanthropic contributions](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26316/w26316.pdf). These files are tracked and evaluated through separate, highly privileged channels.

### **Which colleges do not consider legacy admissions?**

A growing list of top-tier colleges do not consider legacy status at all. Some, like MIT and Caltech, have never used legacy preferences. Others, including Johns Hopkins, Amherst, Wesleyan, Carnegie Mellon, and the public university systems of California, Virginia, Colorado, and Maryland, have formally abolished legacy admissions.

For families navigating this shifting landscape, the path to admissions success remains anchored in individual character and preparation.
