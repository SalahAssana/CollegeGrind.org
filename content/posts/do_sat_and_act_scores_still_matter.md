The landscape of American higher education admissions has experienced an [unprecedented policy shift](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/). Following the rapid, emergency adoption of test-optional policies during the COVID-19 pandemic, higher education entered an era characterized by institutional divergence. While over 90% of U.S. four-year degree-granting institutions—representing more than 1,800 colleges—continue to operate under [test-optional or test-free policies](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/), a critical mass of highly selective research universities [reinstated mandatory standardized testing requirements](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/).

This research report investigates whether SAT and ACT scores still matter in college admissions. Based on an empirical analysis of institutional admissions data, peer-reviewed predictive validity studies, organizational tax filings, and state scholarship statutes, the evidence supports a nuanced central conclusion: SAT and ACT scores remain highly consequential at specific institutions and for specific applicant demographics, but they are no longer universally necessary or universally beneficial. Standardized testing must be understood not as a blanket hurdle, but as a strategic asset that carries variable weight depending on a college’s policy, an applicant’s academic profile, and [specific financial aid formulas](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges).

Key findings of this investigation include:

* **Selective Reinstatement Driven by Empirical Research**: Highly selective private and public universities—including Harvard, Yale, Dartmouth, Brown, Cornell, the University of Pennsylvania, MIT, Caltech, Stanford, Georgetown, and the University of Texas at Austin—[reinstated mandatory SAT/ACT requirements](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/). Internal institutional research at these universities revealed that test scores remain the single strongest predictor of college GPA and persistence, especially when evaluating applicants across high schools affected by severe grade inflation.
* **The Withholding Paradox Among Disadvantaged Applicants**: Internal data from Dartmouth, Yale, and Brown demonstrated that [test-optional policies inadvertently harmed low-income and first-generation applicants](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/). Misinterpreting test-optional middle 50% score ranges, high-achieving applicants from lower-resourced high schools frequently withheld scores (such as a 1400 SAT) that would have substantially increased their likelihood of admission when evaluated within their local high school context.
* **The Test-Optional Admissions Disparity**: At selective colleges maintaining test-optional policies, [score submitters are admitted at significantly higher rates](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/) than non-submitters. While this disparity is partially driven by selection bias—as score submitters often possess stronger academic portfolios overall—submitting a competitive test score provides [positive academic confirmation](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges) that non-submitters must match through other application components.
* **The Disconnect Between Admissions and Merit Scholarships**: A college's admissions policy does not dictate its financial aid policy. Numerous public universities operate test-optional admissions processes while maintaining mandatory SAT/ACT score thresholds for [automatic, non-need-based merit scholarships](https://afford.ua.edu/scholarships/oos-international-freshman/). Furthermore, state-funded scholarship programs, such as the [Florida Bright Futures program](https://www.floridastudentfinancialaidsg.org/PDF/FAS-FMS.pdf), mandate test score minimums established by state legislation.
* **Evolving Financial Models in the Testing Industry**: The [business models governing standardized testing](https://mhstheunion.com/2025/10/02/collegeboard-is-not-as-non-profitable-as-you-think/) have shifted dramatically. While the College Board remains a non-profit 501(c)(3) organization generating over $1.1 billion in annual revenue—driven primarily by its Advanced Placement (AP) program—the ACT organization transitioned from a non-profit entity in 2024 into a for-profit Public Benefit Corporation acquired by private equity firm Nexus Capital Management, and subsequently [acquired by the Educational Testing Service (ETS)](https://learnworkecosystemlibrary.com/organizations/act/) in 2026.

The post-COVID era initially suggested a permanent decline in standardized testing. However, beginning in early 2024 and accelerating through 2026, elite universities [abandoned temporary test-optional waivers and restored testing mandates](https%3A%2F%2Forieladmissions.com%2Fcolleges-require-sat-act%2F).

## **Defining the Policy Taxonomy**

Navigating college admissions requires precise understanding of university testing terminology. Admissions offices utilize specific administrative categories that dictate how test scores are processed4:

### **Test-Required**

An application will not be reviewed until official or verified self-reported SAT or ACT scores are received. Test scores are evaluated alongside high school transcripts, essays, letters of recommendation, and extracurricular activities. Examples include [MIT, Harvard, Yale, Dartmouth, Brown](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/), [Cornell, Penn](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/), [Stanford, Caltech](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/), [Georgetown, UT Austin](https://jorbi.ai/blog/which-colleges-require-sat-act-in-2026-2027/), and [all public universities in Florida](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/colleges-that-require-sat-act), Georgia, and Tennessee.

### **Test-Optional**

Applicants choose whether to submit SAT or ACT scores. If an applicant withholds scores, the admissions committee evaluates the file based on the remaining components without penalty. However, test-optional does not mean test-blind; when submitted, competitive test scores serve as [positive academic data points](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges). Examples include the [University of Chicago](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges), [Duke](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/), Northwestern, and the [University of Michigan](https://www.voicedacademy.com/blog/which-colleges-require-sat-act-2026-2027).

### **Test-Preferred and Test-Encouraged**

A designation within test-optional admissions where an institution explicitly signals that [score submission is advantageous](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges). While applications without scores remain complete, colleges using this language indicate that scores help confirm academic readiness. Examples include Rice University, Tulane University, and Notre Dame.

### **Test-Flexible**

Institutions require standardized assessment data but allow candidates to fulfill the requirement using alternative examinations, such as Advanced Placement (AP) or International Baccalaureate (IB) scores, in place of the SAT or ACT. While [Yale University](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/) briefly utilized a test-flexible model, it returned to requiring the SAT or ACT outright. Carnegie Mellon University maintains [test-flexible options](https://www.cmu.edu/admission/admission/standardized-testing) across select academic programs.

### **Test-Free / Test-Blind**

Institutions will not review or consider standardized test scores under any circumstances, even if an applicant submits maximum scores. The [University of California system](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges) (including UC Berkeley and UCLA) and the California State University system operate under permanent test-free mandates.

## **Why Selective Universities Reinstated Standardized Testing**

The decisions by Dartmouth, Harvard, Brown, Yale, MIT, Caltech, Stanford, and Penn to end test-optional admissions were informed by [internal institutional research](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/). Rather than reflecting ideological shifts, these policy reversals were driven by empirical data regarding student performance, grade inflation, and equity.

### **The Calibration of Widespread Grade Inflation**

Secondary school grading practices have shifted over the past decade. High school GPAs have clustered heavily in the 3.8 to 4.0 unweighted range across diverse secondary schools, reducing the transcript's ability to differentiate candidate preparation. Standardized test scores provide a common, objective metric across more than 26,000 U.S. high schools, allowing admissions officers to calibrate GPAs against a national distribution.

### **Empirical Predictive Validity in College Performance**

Institutional research across reinstated universities confirmed that SAT and ACT scores remain strong predictors of college academic performance. A landmark 2024 study by Opportunity Insights—led by Harvard economists Raj Chetty, David Deming, and John Friedman—analyzed data from elite colleges and concluded that SAT/ACT scores were far more predictive of college GPA, persistence, and post-graduation success than high school GPA. Dartmouth’s internal research demonstrated that incorporating test scores alongside high school GPA increased the explained variance in first-year college academic performance from under 15% to over 25%.

### **The Disadvantaged Applicant Withholding Paradox**

The primary equity justification for test-optional admissions was the assumption that dropping test requirements would increase enrollment among low-income, first-generation, and underrepresented minority students. However, internal studies at Dartmouth, Yale, and Brown revealed an unintended consequence: disadvantaged applicants frequently withheld test scores that would have significantly increased their likelihood of admission.

Because published test-optional middle 50% score ranges rose rapidly due to self-selection, applicants assumed that any score below a school's published 25th percentile (such as a 1400 SAT at an Ivy League institution) would harm their application. In practice, admissions officers evaluating applicants within their local high school context viewed a 1400 SAT from a school with a median SAT of 1000 as an indicator of academic excellence. By withholding those scores under test-optional rules, disadvantaged students deprived admissions committees of key contextual data, leading to lower admission rates for non-submitters in lower income brackets.

## **Why the Majority of Colleges Maintain Test-Optional and Test-Free Models**

While elite research institutions have led the return to mandatory testing, the majority of American colleges maintain [test-optional or test-free models](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/). Understanding why broad-access public flagships and regional liberal arts colleges preserve these policies highlights differing institutional priorities across higher education sectors.

### **Application Volume and Enrollment Management**

For many regional public universities and private liberal arts colleges, test-optional policies lower application barriers, leading to [increases in total application volume](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/). Higher application volume allows institutions to be more selective, manage enrollment yields effectively, and [generate additional revenue from application fees](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges).

### **Fulfilling Regional Access Mandates**

Colleges with broad educational access mandates view standardized tests as [barriers for historically underrepresented, low-income, and rural populations](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/). For institutions focused on regional workforce development rather than elite sorting, eliminating test requirements aligns with institutional equity goals and helps maintain target enrollment levels.

### **Comprehensive Holistic Evaluation Capacities**

Institutions that adopted test-optional admissions long before the pandemic—such as [Bowdoin College](https://neirad.org/12514/opinion/the-story-of-test-optional/) (test-optional since 1969), [Bates College](https://www.bates.edu/admission/apply/optional-testing/) (1984), and the [University of Chicago](https://orieladmissions.com/does-uchicago-require-the-sat/) (2018)—have developed holistic review models that evaluate candidate potential without standardized metrics. These colleges report that multi-year cohorts admitted without test scores [graduate and achieve academic outcomes](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges) at rates comparable to score-submitting peers.

### **Transcript Validation and Grade Confirmation**

The primary function of an SAT or ACT score is to [validate classroom performance](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/). A high cumulative GPA (3.9+) accompanied by a strong test score (1500+ SAT / 34+ ACT) confirms academic preparation. Conversely, a high GPA paired with a lower test score prompts admissions officers to examine high school course selection and grading standards more closely.

### **Contextualized Score Review**

Admissions officers evaluate standardized test scores in the context of the applicant's educational environment. Environmental context tools—such as the [College Board's Environmental Context Dashboard data](https://secure-media.collegeboard.org/pdf/environmental-context-dashboard-faqs.pdf)—provide admissions staff with aggregate data regarding a student’s high school, including:

* [Median SAT/ACT scores of the graduating class](https://www.aralia.com/helpful-information/test-optional-2026-2027/).
* Percentage of students eligible for free or reduced-price lunch.
* [Total number of Advanced Placement (AP), International Baccalaureate (IB), or Honors courses offered](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges).

Under contextualized review, a 1380 SAT from a student attending a high school where the median score is 980 represents exceptional achievement. That same 1380 SAT from an applicant attending an elite private school where the average score is 1450 provides a different academic signal.

## **Disentangling Test-Optional Admissions: Submitters vs. Non-Submitters**

A central question for applicants navigating test-optional colleges is whether non-submitters face an [admissions disadvantage](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges). While colleges state that applying test-optionally carries no explicit penalty, empirical data from Common Application reports and university disclosures reveal [consistent differences in admission outcomes](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/).

At competitive test-optional institutions, students who submit test scores are admitted at higher rates than non-submitters. Data from the Common Application indicates that approximately 80% to 85% of enrolled students at top-30 test-optional universities submitted test scores during recent admissions cycles. At Boston College, 75% of admitted students submitted test scores.

Higher admission rates among score submitters do not conclusively prove that submitting a score [causes better admissions outcomes](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/). A significant portion of this disparity is driven by selection bias: applicants who achieve high SAT or ACT scores also tend to possess stronger academic records, higher class ranks, greater course rigor, and better-resourced extracurricular portfolios. However, submitting a high test score provides clear positive value by [eliminating uncertainty regarding transcript rigor](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges).

### **Step-by-Step Decision Protocol**

1. **Above the 50th Percentile**: [Submit score](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges). The score falls in the upper half of enrolled students, serving as a positive academic data point.
2. **25th to 50th Percentile**: [Evaluate high school context and major](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges). If the score is significantly higher than the high school’s average score, or if the applicant is applying to a non-STEM major at a test-optional institution, [submitting the score is generally advantageous](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/).
3. **Below the 25th Percentile**: [Consider withholding](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges). Scores below the published 25th percentile may signal lower academic preparation relative to the applicant pool. Non-submission is recommended unless the student’s educational background [provides exceptional context](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/).

High school GPA captures non-cognitive skills—such as time management, consistency, and classroom engagement—that are essential for long-term academic success. Institutional research shows that combining high school GPA with standardized test scores yields the highest predictive accuracy, increasing the explained variance in first-year college performance significantly compared to evaluating GPA alone.

## **Comprehensive Assessment: Arguments For and Against Testing**

The ongoing debate over standardized testing highlights conflicting priorities across access, equity, and academic evaluation.

### **Comprehensive Criticism (The Case Against)**

1. **Socioeconomic Inequality**: Standardized test scores correlate with [family income, parental education levels, and zip code resources](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202601.0222). Higher-income families can invest in [expensive college readiness services](https://hayestalisman.com/1641/news/the-real-costs-of-expensive-college-readiness-services/), private tutoring, and repeated test sittings, creating structural advantages.
2. **Narrow Skill Measurement**: Standardized tests measure a specific subset of mathematical reasoning, reading comprehension, and writing mechanics. They do not evaluate creativity, critical thinking, leadership, persistence, or specialized artistic talent.
3. **Test Anxiety and Neurodiversity**: High-stakes testing environments can induce anxiety that degrades score accuracy for certain students. Neurodivergent students or those with learning differences may face administrative barriers when seeking testing accommodations.
4. **Geographic and Physical Access Barriers**: Students in rural areas, low-income urban districts, or foreign countries often encounter limited test center availability, cancellations, or transportation challenges.

### **Empirical Defenses (The Case For)**

1. **National Standardization Metric**: Standardized tests offer an objective evaluation tool that [counteracts national grade inflation](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/) and wide variations in high school grading standards.
2. **Identifying High-Performing Disadvantaged Students**: Standardized tests help admissions committees [identify academically talented students](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/) from under-resourced high schools that lack AP, IB, or advanced coursework. A strong score provides evidence of academic readiness that might otherwise remain hidden on an uncalibrated transcript.
3. **Objective Benchmark Against Subjective Bias**: Proponents argue that standardized test scores provide a transparent metric that shields admissions evaluations from subjective biases inherent in polished personal essays, counselor letters, or paid extracurricular achievements.

## **Disadvantaged Students and Standardized Testing: The Context Paradox**

Standardized testing functions as a double-edged sword for socioeconomically disadvantaged applicants. While family income correlates with [average SAT/ACT scores](https://mhstheunion.com/2025/10/02/collegeboard-is-not-as-non-profitable-as-you-think/), eliminating test scores removes a key mechanism by which high-achieving low-income students [demonstrate academic readiness](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/).

Admissions research at Dartmouth, Yale, and MIT revealed that [test-optional policies reduced admissions accuracy](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/) for low-income applicants. In under-resourced high schools offering no Advanced Placement or International Baccalaureate courses, a student’s 4.0 GPA provides limited information regarding preparation for rigorous college coursework. A standardized score of 1350 SAT—while below an Ivy League university’s overall median—ranks in the top percentile of that student's high school cohort, providing strong evidence of academic talent. When test scores are eliminated, admissions committees must rely more heavily on non-academic factors—such as polished essays, high-cost extracurricular involvement, and counselor recommendations—areas where [affluent applicants hold significant advantages](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/).

### **The Test Preparation Ecosystem**

* **Free Practice Resources**: The College Board’s partnership with Khan Academy offers free, personalized SAT preparation. Official College Board data indicates that [20 hours of personalized practice on Khan Academy](https://newsroom.collegeboard.org/new-data-links-20-hours-personalized-official-sat-practice-khan-academy-115-point-average-score) is associated with an average score gain of 115 points over PSAT baselines. Independent studies indicate that marginal gains directly attributable to practice platforms typically average 50 to 100 points.
* **Commercial Courses and Tutoring**: Commercial courses and private tutoring yield variable score increases. While structured tutoring helps students master test formats and timing, research indicates that commercial prep primarily optimizes test-taking strategies rather than fundamentally altering underlying academic aptitude.

### **Direct Financial Costs and Fee Waivers**

Direct testing costs include registration fees (~$68 for the SAT; ~$69–$94 for the ACT), late registration fees, and score report fees. Income-eligible students can obtain [fee waivers covering full registration costs](https://www.causeiq.com/organizations/the-college-board,131623965/) for up to two sittings, unlimited score reports, and college application fee waivers.

### **Marginal Value of Time**

Time dedicated to test preparation carries an [opportunity cost](https://hayestalisman.com/1641/news/the-real-costs-of-expensive-college-readiness-services/). If an applicant allocates 100 hours to test preparation to raise an SAT score from 1420 to 1480, that investment provides high marginal value when applying to [test-required or merit-scholarship institutions](https://orieladmissions.com/colleges-require-sat-act/). Conversely, spending 100 hours attempting to raise an SAT score from 1150 to 1220 while applying exclusively to test-optional schools with median SATs of 1400 offers a lower return than investing those hours in high school coursework or [extracurricular leadership](https://www.crimsoneducation.org/us/blog/test-optional-colleges).

A common strategic mistake occurs when applicants assume that a university's test-optional admissions policy extends to institutional and state merit scholarship programs. Many colleges utilize test-optional policies for admissions decisions while maintaining test-score requirements for [merit scholarship distribution](https://www.floridastudentfinancialaidsg.org/PDF/FAS-FMS.pdf).

### **Institutional Merit Formulas**

Public state universities use standardized test scores alongside GPA metrics to calculate automatic merit scholarship tiers.

* **University of Alabama**: While maintaining test-optional admissions, Alabama [requires test scores for its automatic Out-of-State Freshman Merit Scholarships](https://afford.ua.edu/scholarships/oos-international-freshman/). An applicant with a 3.5+ unweighted GPA and a 32–36 ACT (1420–1600 SAT) automatically qualifies for the Presidential Scholarship, valued at $28,000 per year ($112,000 over four years). An applicant with a 4.0+ GPA and a 36 ACT / 1600 SAT receives the Presidential Elite Scholarship, covering full tuition, housing, and stipends. Non-submitters remain eligible only for smaller, competitive merit pools.
* **University of Texas at Dallas**: UT Dallas operates a test-optional admissions process but encourages score submission for its [Academic Excellence Scholarship (AES) program](https://enroll.utdallas.edu/affordability/freshman-scholarships/academic-excellence-scholarship/aes-for-prospective-freshmen/), where high test scores enhance award consideration.

### **State-Sponsored Merit Programs**

State financial aid programs often enforce statutory testing thresholds set by state legislatures.

* **Florida Bright Futures Scholarship Program**: Florida high school graduates must satisfy test score minimums established by the state legislature. The Florida Academic Scholars (FAS) award—which covers 100% of tuition and mandatory fees at Florida public universities—requires a [1330 SAT / 29 ACT for 2026–2027 graduates](https://www.floridastudentfinancialaidsg.org/PDF/FAS-FMS.pdf) alongside a 3.50 weighted GPA and 100 service hours. The Florida Medallion Scholars (FMS) award (75% tuition) mandates an 1190 SAT / 24 ACT.

### **The College Board: Non-Profit Financial Profile**

The College Board is registered as a 501(c)(3) non-profit organization. Under federal tax law, 501(c)(3) status exempts the organization from federal corporate income tax in exchange for serving an educational mission. Non-profit status does not preclude the generation of net income or executive compensation.

Key financial data from College Board IRS Form 990 tax filings reveals:

* **Total Annual Revenue**: Generates approximately **$1.1 billion** in annual total revenue.
* **Primary Revenue Driver (AP Program)**: The Advanced Placement (AP) program has surpassed the SAT as the primary source of revenue. The AP program generates over **$430 million** annually in exam fee revenue.
* **Executive Compensation**: According to Schedule J of IRS Form 990, Chief Executive Officer David Coleman received total compensation exceeding **$2.3 million**.
* **Fee Waiver Expenditures**: Allocated over **$106 million** in 2024 toward fee waivers and reductions for low-income candidates across its programs.

### **ACT, Inc.: Transition to For-Profit Structure**

The organization behind the ACT underwent structural transformations that altered its operating model.

* **Private Equity Acquisition (2024)**: Founded in 1959 as an independent non-profit organization, ACT, Inc. was acquired in April 2024 by private equity firm **Nexus Capital Management LP**. Following the acquisition, ACT transitioned into a for-profit **Public Benefit Corporation (PBC)**.
* **Acquisition by ETS (2026)**: In June 2026, the Educational Testing Service (ETS) acquired ACT, Inc. while electing to preserve ACT’s for-profit operating structure. The combined entity serves millions of learners globally.

These corporate structures illustrate that both testing organizations operate with financial incentives to maintain testing participation across high schools and higher education.

## **Testing Strategy Across Special Student Populations**

Determining a standardized testing strategy varies across candidate populations:

* **Homeschooled Applicants**: Because admissions committees cannot evaluate a traditional high school profile or calibrated grading scale, standardized test scores provide critical evidence of academic preparation. Homeschooled students are strongly advised to submit SAT or ACT scores—often alongside AP exams or dual-enrollment credits—to validate home-based coursework.
* **International Applicants**: While an SAT or ACT score provides admissions committees with a familiar evaluation metric across unfamiliar international grading systems, standardized tests do not satisfy English language proficiency requirements at most institutions. International students from non-English speaking countries must submit separate English proficiency exams (TOEFL, IELTS, or Duolingo English Test) regardless of SAT/ACT submission status.
* **Applicants from Under-Resourced High Schools**: For candidates attending high schools with limited advanced coursework, a strong test score demonstrates academic capability that might not be evident on an uncalibrated transcript.
* **Transfer Students**: Transfer admissions focus primarily on post-secondary academic performance. Students who have completed 24 or more college credit hours are evaluated on their college GPA and coursework, with high school SAT/ACT scores carrying minimal weight or waived entirely.

## **SAT vs. ACT: Strategic Assessment**

When choosing between the SAT and ACT, applicants should evaluate test design differences rather than perceived admissions preferences. Colleges accept both examinations equally, converting results using official concordance tables.

### **Structural Format Differences**

* **Digital SAT**: Utilizes a multistage adaptive assessment model divided into Reading/Writing and Math modules. The test lasts 2 hours and 14 minutes, features shorter reading passages with one question per passage, and permits a graphing calculator across all math sections.
* **Updated ACT**: The enhanced ACT features a core linear structure lasting **2 hours and 5 minutes** (125 minutes of testing time without optional sections). The core test comprises English, Math, and Reading, with Math options revised to four choices per question instead of five. The **Science section is optional** (adding 40 minutes if taken) and does not factor into the main 1–36 composite score, giving candidates flexibility based on their individual strengths and program prerequisites.

### **Superscoring Policies**

Superscoring combines an applicant's highest individual section scores across multiple test sittings to calculate a composite score. Most institutions—including MIT, Yale, Harvard, Brown, Penn, Stanford, and Dartmouth—superscore both the SAT and ACT. Exceptions exist: Georgetown University requires full testing history and does not calculate section superscores.

## **Practical Decision Framework and Action Plan**

High school students should follow an 8-step decision protocol when evaluating standardized testing:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐  
│                      EIGHT-STEP STUDENT ACTION PROTOCOL                     │  
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤  
│ Step 1: Map Institutional Policies across your target college list.         │  
│ Step 2: Identify Merit Aid Score Minimums for state and college awards.     │  
│ Step 3: Establish Baseline Scores via full-length practice tests.           │  
│ Step 4: Execute Focused Prep (20–40 hours on official practice platforms).  │  
│ Step 5: Sit for Initial Examination during junior year.                     │  
│ Step 6: Compare Final Scores against Common Data Set Section C9 ranges.     │  
│ Step 7: Target Retakes strategically if superscoring offers advantages.     │  
│ Step 8: Finalize Submission Decisions separately for each target college.   │  
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

```

1. **Map Institutional Policies**: Categorize target colleges as test-required, test-optional, test-preferred, or test-free.
2. **Identify Merit Aid Thresholds**: Determine whether state aid programs (such as [Florida Bright Futures](https://www.floridastudentfinancialaidsg.org/PDF/FAS-FMS.pdf)) or college merit awards enforce minimum testing score cutoffs.
3. **Establish Baseline Performance**: Take a full-length, timed diagnostic practice SAT and ACT to identify natural test strengths.
4. **Execute Focused Preparation**: Allocate 20 to 40 hours of preparation using official materials, prioritizing targeted weak content areas.
5. **Schedule Initial Examination**: Sit for the primary test during junior year, leaving sufficient time for a retake if necessary.
6. **Compare Scores Against Common Data Sets**: Evaluate final scores against Section C9 of the Common Data Set for every college on your list.
7. **Target Retakes Strategically**: If an initial score sits just below an institution's 50th percentile, target specific section improvements for superscoring advantages.
8. **Finalize Submission Decisions**: Make score submission decisions individually for each college on the application list.
